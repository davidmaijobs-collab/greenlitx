The Keeper of Forgotten Things

Everything lost is waiting somewhere

Eleven-year-old Wren Halloway falls through her linen closet into the Hollow — the twilight world where forgotten things wake up alive — and finds Mr. Buttons, the bear she lost, alive there and a stranger. She has until the long dusk ends to be loved by him again for who he is now, before he dissolves into the Mothlight.

Story Overview

Everyone knows where lost things go in stories. Nobody asks about the ones who are not lost, just forgotten: the umbrella left on a train in 1987, the wedding ring taken off by a marriage already over, the imaginary friend of a boy who turned ten. They go to the Hollow — a twilight world grown from everything humanity has stopped remembering, where forgotten objects wake up as living creatures. An umbrella becomes a gangly heron-like bird that still folds itself over anything smaller caught in the rain. A teddy bear becomes something close to a king.

Wren Halloway, eleven, is fierce, funny, and furious at adults who keep saying “grown-up reasons.” Her father is dead. Her mother is undergoing treatment no one will describe to her honestly. Tomorrow she is to be sent to an aunt she barely knows; tonight, the house is half in boxes. Wren manages grief by refusing to lose. If she can keep one thing from her old life she tells herself, the rest cannot really be changing. That one thing is Mr. Buttons, the bear her father gave her. And tonight, Mr. Buttons is missing.

Searching for him, she pushes through the linen closet — and keeps falling. Animation lets the fall do what no live-action fall could: shelves stretch into cliffs of folded sheets, Wren shrinks past buttons the size of cartwheels, and she lands, small, in the Hollow itself — suddenly the most breakable thing in sight. She finds the Nursery Glades, where newly forgotten toys arrive frightened and small. And she finds Mr. Buttons.

He is alive. That is the wonder, and it arrives in the same breath as the wound. Buttons is steward of the Glades, a bear of considerable dignity who receives each frightened newcomer kindly. He is gentle with Wren — attentive, courteous, unhurried. He does not know her. Not her name. Not her father. Not one shared afternoon. Buttons has set his memories down, and what startles Wren most is that he does not appear to suffer for it. He seems at peace. Her hope and the knife in it are the same thing: the bear she came to rescue does not believe he needs rescuing.

The Hollow runs on a law no one made and no one can bribe. A forgotten thing remembered again may return to the world. But a thing that forgets itself entirely thins, thread by visible thread — felt loosening, seams sighing open, colour leaving like breath off glass — and dissolves into the Mothlight, the pale dust tended by the Keeper. The Keeper is the Hollow's shepherd: immense, slow, and kind-eyed, a horizon-sized figure assembled from lost wedding rings, left gloves, unsent letters, and ten thousand imaginary friends. Nothing about it is wicked. That is what makes petitioning it so frightening.

The long dusk — the slow twilight in which this world lives — is ending early, and with the dusk goes Wren's chance to reach Buttons at all. Guided by Pell, the brass fox who remembers everything because a key that forgets its lock would cease to exist, Wren crosses a world that argues with her in landscapes: smouldering battlefields of old arguments, shores of things left behind, and a city of imaginary friends who face the opposite sorrow to Buttons: they remember their children perfectly, forever, and are remembered by no one.

So Wren's task is harder than remembering loudly enough. Anyone can mourn what someone was. The question the Hollow puts to her is whether she can come to love who Buttons is now — this steward, this stranger, this bear whose life no longer includes her — without treating his present self as a locked door with her own childhood behind it. Children will get the wonder first; adults may find the walk back through it less comfortable.

The story

Wren Halloway is eleven, her house is in boxes, and the bear her late father gave her is missing. Searching the linen closet on her last night at home, she falls — out of her size, out of her world — into the Hollow, where forgotten things wake alive: umbrellas as herons, keys as foxes, imaginary friends as citizens. Mr. Buttons is there, kind, dignified, and a complete stranger. Twilight there is ending early. Love, to matter, will have to start again.

The Keeper of Forgotten Things

Development treatment — not a shooting script.

Act I — Setup

The house is ending in cardboard. Wren Halloway, eleven, moves through rooms that are half furniture, half boxes, labelling things: KEEP. KEEP. NOT YOURS. Her father is dead. Her mother is ill, undergoing treatment the grown-ups describe only in weather — “doing well,” “a difficult week,” “being managed.” No one will give her a plain noun. What she knows is the plan: tomorrow she goes to live with an aunt she barely knows. Tonight is the last night in this house.

Wren is not only a grieving child. She is funny, dry, and climbs what should not be climbed. On the kitchen windowsill she keeps rescued cuttings in jam jars — snapped stems other people threw away, coaxed back toward green — and is fiercely proud that two have rooted. Being trusted with something alive, and not breaking it, is her one talent.

What she cannot find tonight is Mr. Buttons, the bear her father gave her, the one object she has flatly refused to pack. If he comes with her, unchanged, the move is only a move. If he does not, everything is true. She searches everywhere, and pushes at last through the linen closet.

The closet does not end. Animation makes the fall the film's thesis in motion: shelves stretch into cliffs of folded white, and Wren shrinks as she falls — past a button the size of a cartwheel — until she lands, child-sized in a world built to object scale, in the Hollow. Nothing here keeps the shape it arrived in. Umbrellas have become heron-like birds, ribs opened into wings, handles curved into patient necks, still trying to shelter whatever stands beneath them.

Pell finds her first — or fails to hide from her first: a small brass fox with a key's teeth along his belly, clicking softly at every joint, a door-key from a demolished house still, after a century, trying to get home. He is comic, cowardly, and the Hollow's secret historian. A key's nature is to fit one specific lock, so Pell cannot forget without ceasing to exist. He remembers everything. It has made him tired, funny, and a compulsive warner of people who never listen.

Pell leads her, warning all the way, to the Nursery Glades, where newly forgotten toys arrive frightened and small under the Rain of Small Hours — a gentle rain of tiny glowing moments, each a minute somebody lived and lost. There, receiving the newcomers, is Mr. Buttons, grown into his dignity: a great bear, gathering a trembling new doll against his chest. Wren says his name. He turns, looks at her with real kindness — the kindness he gives everyone — and asks, courteously, whether she is newly arrived, and whether there is anything he can do.

He does not know her. Not her name, not her father, not one afternoon. The promised reunion is a non-recognition scene played at wonder's scale: the bigger and more beloved Buttons has become, the more completely he is someone else. Wren refuses. She recites — birthdays, illnesses, her father's voice doing Buttons's voice — and watches each memory land like rain on a coat he is not wearing. Buttons listens with perfect attention; attention, she learns, is not memory. He experiences his forgetting not as a wound but as peace, and when she presses, his courtesy hardens into a steward's formality: he is not, he tells her, a thing to be claimed at the gate. It is his one edge of temper, and it frightens her more than anger would.

Afterwards Pell explains the law. A forgotten thing remembered again may return to the world. But a thing that forgets itself entirely thins and dissolves into the Mothlight — the pale dust tended by the Keeper, the vast shepherd assembled from rings, gloves, unsent letters, and ten thousand imaginary friends. And the long dusk, the Hollow's slow twilight, is ending early. Pell glances away when she asks why. Earlier, at the Glades' edge, he had stopped short of the low threshold there and gone the long way round through the wet grass rather than cross it, his clicking quickening, and he had not explained that either. Wren hears only the deadline: she has until the dusk ends to make Buttons remember her. She asks the wrong question with her whole heart — how does she make him? ### Act II — Escalation

Wren sets out with Pell clicking beside her to petition the Keeper for an exception: Buttons given back his past, as one gives back a coat. Early on, the law stops being exposition. At the Glades' edge, Wren finds a doll newly arrived and inconsolable for her child, and does everything she is planning for Buttons, in miniature and at speed — pours out names, histories, and insists, with love, that she remember. The doll's Hollow self does not triumphantly return. It winks out, mid-word, leaving a drift of Mothlight. Forced remembering does not restore a creature's present self; it ends it. Wren has performed her plan once, on someone else, and seen its cost. She keeps walking anyway.

The Battlefield of Lost Arguments is a plain of warm ash where every unfinished quarrel still smoulders, flaring near old grievances; Wren and Pell cross it bickering, then not speaking, and the ash glows brighter around their silence until Pell apologises first — badly, at length, to the horizon — and it dims. On the Coast of Left-Behinds, tides of gloves, scarves, and single shoes sort themselves forever toward owners who are not coming, and Wren wades through saying no name aloud.

The moral hinge is the City of Imaginary Friends: companions invented by children, grown here to the size of their inventors' love — among them a small, solemn dragon with excellent manners. They remember their children perfectly: every game, every secret, the exact weight of a head on a shoulder in 1963. They are Buttons inverted, and they beg Wren for messages. Tell her I kept the lighthouse lit. Tell him the password was never forgotten. A living child, to them, is a door. Wren nearly says yes a hundred times. What stops her is the doll. To arrive in a grown stranger's life as proof would use these creatures as she used the doll — as instruments against forgetting, whatever it did to the selves they are now. Wren refuses the smuggling and does the harder, smaller thing: she stays a whole dusk-watch as a witness, learns names she cannot deliver anywhere, and promises only that she will remember them herself, as they are, here. It is the first time she loves something in the Hollow without trying to take it home.

Beyond the City, the country thins and quiets. At its centre stands the Keeper, and nothing prepares scale for it, because the Keeper is scale: a shepherd the size of weather, assembled from a million lost things — wedding rings in chiming braids, left gloves for the soft musculature of its hands, unsent letters for its papery hide, ten thousand imaginary friends woven through it. Its one great eye once belonged to the first imaginary friend it ever shepherded, whose name it tended for a thousand years and then, one dusk, could not recall. It kept the eye; it lost the name. The Keeper does not argue. Wren petitions anyway, fiercely and completely. Make him remember me. You tend everything forgotten. Tend this.

The Keeper refuses her, not cruelly, but by showing her its hands. Its fingers are unravelling, thread by luminous thread — and the midpoint reversal opens under Wren's feet. The Keeper is not decaying. It is forgetting deliberately, creature by creature, as an act of mercy considered longer than Wren's species has had words for grief. The Hollow, in its belief, is a warehouse of pain: thousands of creatures suspended in the ache of being half-remembered. A creature that dissolves feels nothing. The kindest shepherd does not guard the flock forever; it lets the flock stop. That is why the dusk is ending early. Wren is looking at her own philosophy inside out, grown to the size of the sky: she cannot let go of anything; the Keeper cannot hold on to anything; both call it love.

An exception, the Keeper tells her, is only the philosophy with a favourite. Instead it gives her the Hollow's hardest truth: a thing can only be remembered truly if it is loved for what it is now — not mourned for what it was. Wren leaves refused, carrying harder knowledge still: Pell finally tells her about Buttons's old ribbon, the token of their bond, which still exists in the Hollow, faded to the colour of late light. Pressed on him — the past made undeniable — it would return her bear, and end, completely, the Buttons the Hollow loves. Light is draining from the Hollow's sky like water from a basin, and Pell begins, at last, his hundred-year secret. ### Act III — Resolution

Pell's lock was the first door of the Hollow. When the first forgotten thing needed somewhere to go, a door was made, and Pell was its key. Everything ever forgotten is held back, in a sense, by that door — and the Keeper, to finish its mercy, needs only to open it, and let forgetting complete itself at once.

The crisis is weather. The Keeper opens the door, and everything forgotten pours through: a millennium of grief as a luminous storm, Mothlight thickening into blizzard. Creatures dissolve en masse, some almost gratefully. And in the breach stands Buttons. In the film's defining image, the great bear plants himself across the opening, gathering the smallest creatures under his chest, hunching his whole felt bulk into a shelter. His seams open into light; a paw comes partly undone into drifting thread as he lifts a trembling toy from the wind and tucks it, gently, behind him. Stewardship is not Buttons's job. It is Buttons.

Wren reaches him with the ribbon in her hand. Everything she came for is available in one gesture: press it on him, make the past undeniable, get her bear back at the price of the bear in front of her, who is spending himself on strangers. Buttons, unravelling, looks down and asks the question she has dreaded and needed in the same breath: “Were you mine?” She answers honestly, and it costs her the whole film. “I was yours. Now I'm just — I'm Wren. And you're Buttons. And I love you now.” She does not use the ribbon. She tells him who she was to him as a fact, not a claim and loves the bear who is actually there.

It is the first time a remembered thing has been released rather than reclaimed, and the law answers in kind. A thing loved for what it is does not have to return, and does not have to dissolve. It can simply stay, chosen. Buttons's seams slow and hold, and the bear, still himself, still memoryless, decides out loud, so that it counts: “Then let this be the first thing I keep.” He does not regain his memories. He begins to keep new ones. The first is her.

The Keeper, vast in the storm, watches a creature choose to remain itself — a third thing, neither clutching nor release — and stops opening the door. It is not argued out of its mercy; it is shown evidence its philosophy had no category for. Stopping costs it everything it had planned: its hands do not re-form, and it will remain unravelled at the edges, tended now by the small creatures it once tended. It must learn, at the size of weather, Wren's lesson: to be held, imperfectly, as it is.

What remains is Pell, and his price is exact. The door stands open, forgetting pressing on it, and the only key in existence is a small brass fox who has been afraid for a century. If he locks the Hollow's door, he spends the one homecoming he has saved himself for: his own door, his demolished house, will never be his to open. Pell looks at the Glades, at the City's distant lights, at a bear holding a doorway shut with his body, and at Wren, who does not ask him. He turns his key — by his own choice, at his own cost — and locks the door, and stays: not because he failed to get home, but because he chose the place that needed him as the place he belongs.

When the long dusk closes, the Hollow exhales what does not belong to forgetting, and a living child does not belong to it. Wren wakes in the linen closet at dawn, on the morning of the move, bear-less. In the last box is Buttons's ribbon. How it came to be there she never learns, and the film does not explain it. It is a new thing to love, not an old one to clutch.

At her aunt's house — a stranger's hallway, a door she has only ever been driven past — Wren does not perform recovery. She asks one practical question: where the light is. Final image: on a windowsill in a room not yet hers, a jam jar holds one of her rescued cuttings, green against the morning, and tied loosely around the jar, so it can be untied any time, is the ribbon. Wren waters the plant. She is learning to keep something alive without keeping it prisoner. Then she goes to find out what breakfast is, in this house, and who makes it.

Main characters

Wren Halloway

Eleven-year-old girl

Fierce, funny, and furious at adults who keep saying "grown-up reasons." She manages grief by refusing to lose. Motivation: recover Mr. Buttons before the move — if she can keep one thing from her old life, she can pretend the rest is not changing. Arc: from grasping to gratitude.

Mr. Buttons

A teddy bear, steward of the Nursery Glades

A bear of considerable dignity, where newly forgotten toys arrive frightened and small. Kind to everyone, remembering no one — he experiences his forgetting not as tragedy but as peace, and comes to love Wren as a new friend: both her hope, and the knife in it.

Pell, the Brass Fox

A house key, the Hollow's oldest creature

A door-key from a demolished house, still trying to get home after a century. Comic, cowardly, and the only creature who remembers everything — a key's nature is to fit one specific lock; he cannot forget without ceasing to exist. Wren's guide, and the film's secret historian.

The Keeper

Shepherd of the Hollow

Immense, slow, kind-eyed, assembled from lost wedding rings, left gloves, unsent letters, and ten thousand imaginary friends. Not a villain — something sadder: it has carried everyone else's forgetting so long it has begun, deliberately, to set things down — including, soon, itself.

Signature Scenes

The Rain of Small Hours

In the Nursery Glades, a rain of tiny glowing lost minutes falls while a great bear receives frightened, newly forgotten toys — and a girl watches the life her bear has built without her.

The Doll Wren Rescues

Wren tries to rescue a newly arrived doll by pouring her whole remembered life back into her — and learns, in one silent instant, what forced remembering actually costs the creature in front of her.

The City of Imaginary Friends

In a city of imaginary friends who remember their children perfectly, Wren is begged to carry messages to the grown-ups who forgot them — and must choose between smuggling their love home and simply staying to witness it.

The Bear in the Breach

As forgetting pours through a doorway of light like a storm, a great bear shelters the smallest creatures of the Hollow under his own unravelling body — sheltering, as he has always done, everyone but himself.
