Children of the Storm

The storm took the map. They redrew it.

After a category-five cyclone cuts their island in half and the adults do not come back from the harbor, six children — the oldest thirteen, the youngest five — set out across the drowned island to reach the lighthouse radio on the far headland, carrying the baby, the medicine, and one rule: nobody crosses water alone.

Story Overview

Sulawesi Sea, the morning after. A category-five cyclone has cut the island of Kasumba in two, and the harbour is wreckage. The adults sailed out before the storm to secure the fleet, and they have not come back. No masts stand in the harbour. The silence is total, ordinary, and wrong, and thirteen-year-old Ratna, the harbour-master's daughter, is the first to climb the harbour rise, count what is not there, and understand that waiting for someone older to take charge means waiting for someone who is not coming.

What she does instead is count. Ratna leads the way she has watched her father run a harbour, by inventory rather than charisma. Six children, the oldest thirteen, the youngest five. A neighbour's baby, who needs shade, feeding and carrying in turns by arms already full. One machete. And in a cooler packed with ice, her classmate Sari's insulin, with perhaps three days before the ice is gone. Eleven kilometres away, across an island the water has rearranged overnight, stands the lighthouse, and in it the radio that is Kasumba's only voice. Ratna writes the inventory down, because a thing written down can be carried and checked. Then she speaks the rule that will govern every step: nobody crosses water alone.

Not everyone agrees that walking is brave. Bima, eleven, is the headman's son, raised to inherit this island and taught that staying put is what steady people owe the missing. Rescue, in his faith, comes to those who hold their ground. Ratna's faith runs the other way: rescue goes to those who walk toward the signal. Their rivalry is never really about who gives orders. It is a contest between two kinds of loyalty, to the adults who are gone, and to each other, argued kilometre by kilometre, through a river that was not there on Tuesday and a road that now staircases down into the sea.

The clock inside the cooler belongs to Sari, ten years old, and Sari keeps it herself. She maintains the insulin log in an exercise book with the steadiness of a ship's officer, noting doses, ice and her own condition in plain, uncomplaining entries. She refuses to be the reason anyone turns back, and that refusal makes her the group's realist and its most protected member at once. A medical deadline carried by the patient herself gives the trek its unsentimental pace: the ice does not care about arguments, weather or courage, and neither, Sari would say, does she.

The youngest walker carries something else. Little Edo is five. He does not understand the scale of what has happened, and his not understanding is the group's oxygen, a reminder that fear is not the only possible response to a ruined world. Edo spent ordinary evenings at the lighthouse before the storm, and what lives in his memory from those evenings matters to the journey's end in ways the older children only partly grasp. What they know is simpler: Edo freezes at water, completely, and under Ratna's rule a frozen child does not get carried through his fear by force. His fear can stop everyone, and everyone has agreed, in advance, that it will.

There are no villains on Kasumba but physics and grief. The water is the antagonist, new rivers, drowned roads, landmarks moved or vanished, the sun working on the cooler hour by hour, and grief walks in quieter forms: in the father Ratna keeps expecting to see on the harbour rise, in the island Bima was raised to inherit and no longer recognises. This is a survival story about competence as a form of love, about children who endure not by becoming small adults, but by doing, carefully and together, the few things children who grew up around harbours, wells and weather know how to do, and by refusing, at every crossing, to leave anyone in the water alone.

The story

The morning after a category-five cyclone cuts their island in two, the adults do not come back from the harbour. Ratna, thirteen, a harbour-master's daughter, counts what remains: six children, a neighbour's baby, one machete, and her classmate Sari's insulin in a cooler with three days of ice. Eleven kilometres of drowned, rearranged island stand between them and the lighthouse radio, Kasumba's only voice. Ratna writes the inventory, speaks the rule, nobody crosses water alone, and leads them out.

Children of the Storm

Development treatment — not a shooting script.

Act I — Setup

The film opens before the trek, in a single ordinary evening the storm will retroactively make precious. At the lighthouse on Kasumba's far headland, the keeper, a treatment-level figure named here as Pak Harun, lets five-year-old Little Edo help him through the lamp room routine: the kettle on, the chair turned to the view, the log opened. As a game, played over many evenings, the keeper has taught Edo a song whose verses carry, in order, the radio's operating sequence, its call sign, the channel steps and the repeat pattern, a lone keeper's insurance that if he were ever incapacitated, someone small who visited often could work the set by singing the steps. Edo thinks it is a song about the keeper's kettle and the ships. The written protocol book lives at the harbour office. The scene, and the bond inside it, are planted in full because the ending will be built from them.

That night the cyclone comes, rendered briefly and practically. Before it, the island's adults, harbour crews, fishers and parents among them, sail out to secure the fleet. Edo stays the night with Ratna's family, as he often does; his parents are among those who sail. Ratna's father, the harbour-master, a treatment-level figure named here as Danu, is the last adult the children see, checking the harbour rise in the rain. The storm cuts the island in two: a channel of new water divides village side from headland side, roads drown, a river appears where no river was on Tuesday, and the evacuation shelter is taken down to its foundations.

Dawn. Ratna, thirteen, climbs the harbour rise as her father taught her to read a morning, and counts the fleet's masts. There are none. She reads the tide line and understands what no one will say: the adults are not coming back on this tide, or the next. Her grief has a specific name and a specific empty place on the rise, and the film lets it land before it lets her move. Then she goes home and does the thing her upbringing gave her instead of a speech. She counts. Six children: herself; Bima, eleven, the headman's son; Sari, ten, her diabetic classmate; Laras, eight; Bayu, seven; and Edo, five. Plus a neighbour's baby, Sari's insulin in a cooler with three days of ice, one machete, and a lighthouse eleven kilometres away whose radio is the island's only voice.

The two middle children and the baby are identified here, at treatment level only, as the ensemble the stated count requires. Laras, eight, is the baby's elder sister: the baby, named here as Arka, nine months old, is hers to read, because she knows his hunger cry from his tired cry, knows what mashed food he will take and how he sleeps, and carries him in a sling in turns with the older children. Bayu, seven, is small and quick-eyed; his work is to walk ahead within sight, spot the markers the storm left standing, and call out what he sees. Neither performs adult feats: Laras's knowledge is a sister's, Bayu's is attention. The baby is not cargo: he needs feeding, shade, changing and sleep, and his needs set rest stops the insulin clock resents.

Bima argues for waiting, and his argument is not foolishness. His father the headman taught him that after a storm the village stays together, keeps to known ground and makes itself findable; staying is a duty owed to the missing. His father's teaching, wells, rain tanks and the old ridge footpath, is the inheritance Ratna lacks. Ratna's counter is arithmetic: three days of ice, no radio in the village, and no one coming to a village they cannot reach. The argument costs the group its first morning, priced honestly: half a day of ice spent before the first step, entered by Sari in her log without comment. Late on the first day the group steps off the drowned road, baby carried, cooler sealed, the rule spoken aloud by all of them together, because a rule only one person says is an order, and a rule everyone says is a promise: nobody crosses water alone.

The trek is mapped day by day. Day one: the staircase road, where the coast road descends step by broken step into the sea and the children pick along its drowned edge in linked pairs; then the first new river, where Edo freezes mid-crossing, in water up to his knees, silent and locked, and the group learns what the rule costs: they do not drag him. Ratna goes back into the water, stands with him, and talks about the keeper's kettle until his feet move. Night one is the roofless schoolhouse, where Sari reads her log aloud in the dark like a ship's report, doses, ice, distance, and where Ratna lies awake counting a third time and cannot make her father's absence come out as a number.

Act II — Escalation

Day two is the day every trusted landmark fails. The road they knew becomes a lagoon. A bridge the older children crossed all their lives is simply not there, and the argument it triggers is the group's lowest point: Bima says the island is telling them to turn back while turning back is still possible; Ratna says islands do not give advice, water does, and the water is rising behind them too. Bima's wells save the morning, his father's teaching locating a rain tank the storm left clean when the village wells are fouled; the film gives the losing boy a competence that keeps everyone alive. Bayu spots the first ridge markers, and Bima leads that stretch, on ground his birthright knows, while Ratna walks behind with the cooler and learns what it costs her not to be in front.

Sari's crisis builds quietly inside the log. Her entries stay exact about doses and ice, but a second column, how she feels, her own invention from the first morning, begins to come back blank, then is quietly ruled through. She is editing herself out of the report, because if her condition becomes the emergency, the group's choices narrow to her, and she refuses to be cargo. Ratna finds the ruled-through column on the second evening and understands she has been reading a log her friend was protecting her from. The confrontation is small and fierce, in the language both girls have: Ratna does not demand feelings; she asks for the true column, as an officer asks for a true report, and Sari, shaking, gives it. Her deterioration is real, manageable for now, and undeniable, and Ratna's avoided decision steps into view: if the ice fails before the lighthouse, leadership will stop meaning counting and start meaning choosing.

The midpoint is the island's evacuation shelter, stocked on every map with a radio, water and supplies. The children reach it at the end of the second day and find it gone to its foundations. In its place, thrown up by the storm, sits a beached fishing boat from the fleet, and in its sealed locker, dry, the adults' harbour log. Read over Ratna's shoulder, the log closes the central argument and answers the opening's questions. The fleet rode out the cyclone at anchor in the lee of the lighthouse cove, the harbour-master's intended return anchorage. Survivors are there, injured, boats damaged, including Ratna's father, alive, his leg broken by a fallen mast, who dictated the last entry before a two-man boat was sent at first light toward the village with the log, water and word that the fleet waited at the cove. The same reading gives them the keeper, too: Pak Harun reached the cove on the storm's first morning, set out for the village, and was turned back by the new channel, with a wrenched knee that keeps him from his tower stairs; he is with the fleet there, alive, and it was he who told the survivors that if anyone could work his radio, it was the boy he had taught the song to. That boat grounded on the new shoreline by the shelter; its crew, unable to cross the new channel on foot, walked the ridge back to the cove, the ridge the children are travelling in reverse. The adults cannot come to the village: the channel runs too fast and deep for injured people and damaged boats to attempt blind, through a coast whose reefs the storm has rearranged. What they need, the log's last line says in the harbour-master's dictated words, is the lighthouse: its lamp and signal, to show the fleet the safe water home. Waiting was never the plan.

Bima reads the log twice. His faith is vindicated and destroyed in the same moment: the adults are alive, his belief in them was right about everything except what to do, and the half day his waiting cost the ice is written in Sari's log a page earlier. The film does not punish him with a speech. He picks up the cooler and walks: faith, converted into labour. Night two is spent in the beached boat's lee, the baby sleeping in the hull's curve, Edo asleep against Bima's side.

Act III — Resolution

Day three, the insulin's last day. The ice in the cooler is water now, cool water still, and Sari's log records it in the same hand as everything else. The final water is the new channel itself, wider and faster than anything they have crossed, running brown between the children and the headland, crossed at its narrowest hour, linked hand to hand exactly as the rule was built for: Ratna at the upstream end breaking the current, the cooler held high and passed hand over hand, Laras with Arka at the chain's centre, Bayu held between Bima and Sari. Halfway across, the current takes Edo's footing. The chain holds because nobody is in the water alone; hands close, the line steadies, and Edo is set back on his feet by other children refusing to let go. They reach the headland drained, with hours of margin rather than days, and climb to the lighthouse.

The keeper is not there. The lamp room is storm-stripped but standing, and the film pays its withheld causality in things rather than exposition: the keeper's oilskin hangs by the door, his boots are gone — exactly as the harbour log, read at the shelter, had already told them: Pak Harun is with the fleet below, alive, his wrenched knee keeping him from his tower stairs, and the boy he taught the song to is the fleet's only hope of working his radio. The radio is intact but procedural: a marine set that must be brought up in sequence, on its proper channel, its call given in the pattern the fleet is listening for, and the protocol book is in a drowned harbour office eleven kilometres behind them. Instead of the book, the lighthouse has Edo, and Edo, at the tower top, is finally and completely afraid: of the height, of the storm the room remembers, of what the older children need from him. He will not sing, and cannot be made to sing. The decisive choice is Ratna's: with Sari's hours running and a fleet waiting below, the leader who has counted and carried for three days chooses to stop counting. They do not rush him.

Instead, Ratna rebuilds the keeper's world from what the storm left: his kettle set where it always stood, his chair turned back to the view, the log opened at his desk, Bima standing where the keeper stood, unhurried, the shape of a calm adult in the room. Nobody mentions the radio. The room becomes an ordinary evening again, assembled out of care as the trek was assembled out of inventory, and Edo, safe as he was safe a hundred times before the storm, begins, half to himself, to sing the kettle song. Verse by verse the song does its hidden work: Ratna follows the sequence the verses carry, bringing the set up as each verse names its step, keying the transmitter in the pattern the final verse repeats. The answer comes before the song ends: a ship's voice, then another, the fleet's survivors at the cove below, close enough that the children run to the gallery and see figures answering a light that has not burned in three days. The keeper stands with a stick for a crutch. Ratna's father sits on an upturned hull, and the film gives Ratna her grief's answer in the plainest form: she waves, and the sitting man raises both arms, broken leg and all, and waves back with his whole body.

The consequences are kept honest. The signal does not teleport anyone home: it marks the safe water, and the fleet's working boats pick their way through the rearranged channel by the lighthouse's guidance as the light fails, a slow, skilled, adult navigation the children watch as an audience, not a crew. Sari is handed over to trained care at the cove, her log complete, true column included. The final scene is the harbour at dusk, boats coming in, Edo asleep on Bima's shoulder, the empty cooler at Ratna's feet. Kasumba's map will have to be redrawn, and everyone on the shore knows whose walk proved the new map first. Ratna opens Sari's exercise book to a clean page and begins to write the island down as it now is. Final image: seven figures and a baby above a harbour filling with lights, and a coastline being drawn by the girl who walked it, the storm's map replaced, line by line, by theirs.

Main characters

Ratna

Eldest of the six, 13

A harbor-master's daughter who can read weather the way adults read news. She takes command by inventory rather than charisma, and her arc is learning that leadership includes the decision she has been avoiding: what to do when the insulin runs out first.

Bima

Headman's son, 11

Raised to inherit an island that no longer exists, Bima believes staying put is what brave people owe the missing. His rivalry with Ratna is really a contest between two kinds of faith — in the adults, and in each other.

Sari

Diabetic classmate, 10

The group's realist, who keeps the insulin log in a exercise book and reports her own condition like a ship's officer. Sari refuses to be the reason anyone turns back, which makes her the reason no one can.

Little Edo

Youngest walker, 5, and keeper of the lighthouse song

He does not understand the scale of what happened, which is the group's oxygen. The lighthouse keeper taught him the signal song as a game; he alone remembers all of it, and he will only sing it when he is not afraid.

Signature Scenes

Counting Masts, Counting Insulin

At dawn a harbour-master's daughter climbs the rise, counts masts that are not there, then goes home and counts insulin instead, and the counting becomes a decision.

Nobody Crosses Water Alone

At the first new river the children speak their rule aloud and cross linked together, until the youngest freezes mid-water and his leader wades back to stand with him.

The Log Read Aloud in the Schoolhouse

In a roofless schoolhouse under open stars, a ten-year-old reads her insulin log aloud like a ship's officer reporting, and the whole group sleeps to the sound of it.

The Lamp Room Made Ordinary

In a storm-stripped lamp room, children rebuild a keeper's ordinary evening, kettle, chair and view, until its smallest keeper feels safe enough to sing the lighthouse awake.
