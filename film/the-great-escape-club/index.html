The Great Escape Club

Retirement was never the plan.

In 1968 six teenagers buried a bottle of whisky and a pact: whoever was left would drink it together at eighty. Now five survivors are residents of the same Leeds retirement home, the sixth is dying in its hospice wing, and management has refused the trip to Cornwall — so the Great Escape Club plans its breakout with military precision, at four miles an hour.

Story Overview

In the summer of 1968, six teenagers on a school trip to Cornwall buried a bottle of whisky under a cairn above a cove and promised, as teenagers do, that whoever was left would dig it up and drink it at eighty. Arthur Bell, who produced the bottle and refused to say where he had got it, insisted it counted. Half a century later, five of the six are still alive, and all five live at Sunnyvale Retirement Home in Leeds. Arthur is dying in Sunnyvale's hospice wing, and he has asked the others for the only thing he still wants: to be taken south and keep the promise while keeping it is still possible.

Sunnyvale is a complete small society, run on rotas, care plans and pilfered biscuits. Arthur's request travels through that machinery and comes back refused. The reasons are real: insurance that does not cover the journey, a medication schedule that cannot be varied, Arthur's fluctuating strength, and a families' consent form nobody's children will sign. The refusals are not villainy. Arthur's daughter loves him and is frightened; told he has weeks, she cannot sign a paper that might spend some of them on a motorway. To the home, Arthur is a set of risks to be managed. To his four friends, he is a person with one appointment left, and the distance between those two descriptions is where the film lives.

So the Great Escape Club convenes in the laundry room, and its membership is its argument. Margaret 'Mags' Ellery, eighty-one, a former headmistress, draws up the escape like a curriculum: three rehearsals, one operation, one dawn departure. Stanley Kowalski, seventy-nine, a retired safe-cracker whose Parkinson's has taken the steadiness from his hands but nothing from his memory, regards Sunnyvale's medication safe with professional contempt; Arthur's medicine travels on an unvarying schedule, and only Stanley can free it without leaving a mark. Frank Doyle, the group's getaway driver in another life, had his licence revoked in 1994 and his eyesight no longer meets the road's standard, so he cannot take the wheel south; what he still owns outright is nerve, route-craft and timing. Nell Brennan, the fifth survivor, quiet where the others are theatrical, holds the current licence and the steadiness the plan needs at the wheel of a borrowed minibus. Every skill the escape requires is one the world retired, and watching it come back into use is the film's central pleasure.

Against them stands Priya Shah, thirty-eight, Sunnyvale's deputy manager and the club's most dangerous opponent because she is good at her job. Drowning in paperwork, she assembles the evidence piece by piece: missing biscuits, a rota that does not match its night, an unusual interest in the medication room. She is not a jailer in a farce. She is an overworked professional who knows exactly what the journey would break and what it could cost Arthur and the home's other residents, and the planning must survive her competence as well as the home's routines. Rehearsals are run at dawn and corrected like lessons. A practice drill entangles the club in the home's own safety machinery. Arthur's good mornings are spent planning; his bad afternoons threaten everything, and the plan is rebuilt, again and again, around his strength.

The comedy is the comedy of capability, played completely straight: five weathered people applying a lifetime of expertise at four miles an hour, in a world that has filed them as cases. Underneath it sits a question the film refuses to soften. A system that protects people from every risk can protect them from living, and a promise kept for more than fifty years may be the last load-bearing wall of a life. Whether friendship, failing bodies and one borrowed minibus can carry them south against a dying man's deadline is the journey the film invites.

The story

In 1968, six teenagers on a school trip to Cornwall buried a bottle of whisky and made a pact: whoever was left would drink it at eighty. Five of them are left, all residents of Sunnyvale Retirement Home in Leeds, and one of them, Arthur Bell, is dying in its hospice wing. Management has refused the journey south. So the five plan a breakout using the only skills retirement never took: a headmistress's timetable, a safe-cracker's memory, and a convoy that travels at four miles an hour.

Act I — Setup

The film opens in Cornwall in the summer of 1968, in late light above a cove. Six teenagers on a school trip have climbed away from their teachers to a cairn on the headland, and Arthur Bell, sixteen and already the group's ringleader in all matters of ceremony, produces a bottle of whisky from his rucksack and refuses, with great dignity, to say where he got it. The bottle is buried under the cairn, and the pact is Arthur's wording, voted on and carried unanimously: whoever is left will come back at eighty, dig it up, and drink it together. Margaret Ellery, the eldest of the six and already incapable of leaving anything unorganised, makes them repeat it properly. Stanley Kowalski, the quiet one, chooses the stone that marks the place and memorises the cairn the way he will later memorise vaults. Nobody on the headland expects the promise to be tested. That is what makes it binding.

Half a century later, five of the six are still alive, and all five are residents of Sunnyvale Retirement Home in Leeds. The sixth member of the group died some years before the film opens, and the survivors keep the absence the way old friends do, without ceremony and without forgetting. Arthur Bell, now eighty, the age the pact named, is dying in Sunnyvale's hospice wing. His consultant gives him weeks, not months. Margaret 'Mags' Ellery, eighty-one, runs her retirement the way she ran her school, by timetable, rehearsal and whistle. Stanley, seventy-nine, lives with Parkinson's: his hands shake and his memory does not. Frank Doyle, once the fastest driver any of them knew, had his licence revoked in 1994 and his eyesight has since fallen below the standard the road requires. Nell Brennan, the fifth survivor, is the group's quiet centre, the one who notices what the theatrical members miss. All five are filed by Sunnyvale as care plans, medication rounds and risk assessments, and the film's first act is precise about what that filing costs: Arthur is a patient with a prognosis, and he is running out of time in which to be anything else.

Arthur makes his request simply and in person. He asks to be taken to Cornwall, to the cairn, to dig up the bottle and drink it with whoever is left, as promised. The request enters Sunnyvale's machinery and the machinery answers as it is built to answer. Management refuses: the home's insurance does not cover such a journey, Arthur's medication is held in the medication safe and given on a schedule no one may vary, and the families' consent form that would be needed for any exceptional trip comes back unsigned. The refusal's human face is Arthur's daughter Clare, who visits, who loves him, and who will not sign. She has sat with the consultant's weeks-not-months prognosis and done the arithmetic frightened children do: a motorway journey might spend days her father does not have, in a car park or a hospital, among strangers. Her refusal is not cruelty and the film never treats it as such; it is love expressed as protection, which is exactly the force the club is up against. The same afternoon the formal refusal is issued, Mags reads it aloud in the day room, folds it once, and says, "Right. We do it the other way." That night, in the laundry room, over pilfered biscuits, the Great Escape Club holds its founding meeting, and Mags unveils the plan as a curriculum: three rehearsals, one operation, one dawn departure, and every task assigned to the person whose life trained them for it.

Act II — Escalation

The caper is built the way Mags built school terms, in rehearsals that are funny because nobody in the room finds them funny. The first operation is the medication safe. Arthur's medicine cannot simply be requested, because the journey does not officially exist; it must be taken, in the right quantities and in the right order, without a mark left on the safe and without a gap in the record that a night check would catch. Stanley studies the safe the way he once studied vaults and delivers his professional verdict that it is an insult. His rehearsal opening is the club's first triumph: hands shaking, memory flawless, talking Frank through the sequence by number while Nell times him and Mags takes notes like minutes. The second rehearsal tests transport at dawn. The mobility scooters that will carry the club out of Sunnyvale have a range of nine miles, which Frank, who cannot legally drive the road south and knows it, turns from a humiliation into the plan's architecture: the scooters are the breakout leg only, a slow convoy through sleeping Leeds streets to a rendezvous where a minibus, borrowed from a visiting community group, will take the club south, the scooters loaded aboard. Nell, the one member holding a current licence, will drive the minibus; Frank will ride beside her as navigator, route-planner and keeper of timings, the job his eyes and his record still allow him to be the best in the world at. The third rehearsal is a fire drill the club calls to test its dawn timings, which triggers a real evacuation of the whole home and very nearly exposes everything: residents in dressing gowns on the forecourt, staff counting heads, and the club's rehearsal indistinguishable from the emergency it accidentally became.

As the plan sharpens, so does the opposition, and both wear the same face. Priya Shah, Sunnyvale's deputy manager, thirty-eight and drowning in the compliance paperwork that keeps the home lawful, begins assembling evidence piece by piece: biscuits missing from the kitchen, a rota altered in a hand that is not quite the night manager's, Stanley's interest in the medication room, scooter batteries charged at hours no appointment explains. She is not a villain and the film is careful with her caseload: she is responsible for every resident in the building, including the ones whose relatives telephone, and an unauthorised journey by a dying man is, on paper, precisely the catastrophe her job exists to prevent. At the same time, word of the club leaks the way everything leaks in an institution, and helping it becomes Sunnyvale's open secret. A night porter begins charging scooter batteries without being asked. A cook's biscuit tin empties at a rate no one records. Residents who are not members take up small duties, watching corridors and misremembering, on request, what they saw. The conspiracy widens from five people to a building, and each new helper raises both the club's chances and the size of the fall if Priya acts.

Mags's failure, when it comes, is not incompetence but its opposite. Arthur has a bad afternoon, grey and exhausted, and asks, mildly, whether the departure could be moved a day. Mags, deep in the timetable, answers the request as a logistics problem and talks over him, rescheduling his rest, his visitors and his medicine as though he were a difficult room booking. Arthur lets her finish, then observes that the home talks about him in exactly the same tone. It is the film's quietest scene and its turning point for its commander. That evening Mags tears the departure page out of the plan in front of the club, and rebuilds the plan around Arthur's strength instead of against it: departure set by his good hours, rest stops that are his to shorten or extend, and one standing order above all others, that any member, Arthur first, can halt the whole operation with a word. She does not become less of a commander. She learns what the command is for.

The midpoint arrives with Priya's file complete. She summons Mags and lays out the whole case, the safe, the scooters, the minibus, the dawn departure, proved to a standard that would end careers and possibly bring prosecutions. Then she does the thing the club never rehearsed for. She has read Arthur's full record, fifty years of a life reduced to care notes, assessments and an unsigned form, and at midnight she comes to the laundry room carrying the one document the club could not obtain: Arthur's complete medication schedule, dose by dose and hour by hour, the timetable that makes the journey survivable rather than reckless. She red-pencils two fatal flaws in Mags's plan, a medication gap on the second morning and a rest stop placed where no help could reach them, corrects both in a manager's neat hand, and walks out without signing anything. She has not joined the club, and she has not forgiven it. She has decided, at a price she can calculate exactly, her career, her registration, her liability if a dying man is harmed, not to see what she has seen. From that night the plan the club executes is partly hers, and everyone in the laundry room understands that she is now the member in the most danger.

Act III — Resolution

The breakout happens at dawn, and it is executed at four miles an hour. Five residents leave Sunnyvale through the waking streets of Leeds in a mobility-scooter convoy, Frank leading on his scooter and setting a pace of ceremonial slowness, Mags riding order-keeper, Stanley with the travelling medication supply secured exactly as the schedule directs, Nell riding last in line to the rendezvous where the borrowed minibus waits. The scooters are loaded aboard, Arthur is settled with a care that is all tenderness disguised as procedure, and the minibus turns south with Nell at the wheel and Frank navigating beside her from a route he has memorised in three variants. For the first hours the journey is the film's happiest passage: service-station teas, a dying man watching England pass at the speed he chooses, five old friends doing the thing they said they would do. Then the world notices. A concerned motorist reports the convoy; the story becomes local news, then a police welfare check, then, in the headlines, pensioners on the run. Families threaten the home with lawyers, and Clare, seeing her father's face in a news bulletin, understands before anyone tells her where the minibus is going and why she was never going to be able to stop it with a form. Priya hears the same bulletins at Sunnyvale — she has known the destination and the timetable since she corrected the plan at midnight — and, with Arthur's next dose coming due, she takes his medication and a blanket and drives south alone, following the welfare-check reports toward the headland.

The crisis comes two hundred miles from Cornwall, in a service-station car park on a hot afternoon. A dose has run late in the day's delays, the heat has done its work, and Arthur slumps, grey and frighteningly quiet, in the minibus doorway. The club faces the choice the whole film has been building, stated plainly by a paramedic's telephone advice and by Nell's common sense: turn back now to a hospital, where Arthur will be made safe, live a little longer, and die a patient among strangers, or press on to the coast with a dying man whose condition may not survive the road. The club begins, helplessly, to debate it around him, until Arthur, lucid and furious at being discussed in the third person, settles it himself. It is his journey, his promise and his risk, and he chooses the coast. Mags, whose plan says turn back, obeys him without argument, and in that obedience her arc completes: the commander stands down, the friend drives on. Nell turns the minibus south again, Frank reads the road like a liturgy, and the welfare check that finally reaches them is stood down by the sight of what it actually finds, not fugitives but a family keeping an appointment.

They reach the Cornish headland at dawn. The cairn is where six teenagers left it, and digging out the bottle takes all of them, labour divided by ability in the film's closing image of the club itself: Stanley directing the work with shaking hands, remembering the marker stone he chose in 1968; Arthur holding the torch from his chair; Mags doing the digging she would once have delegated, on her knees in the wet grass; Frank and Nell lifting the bottle clear. They drink together, one small glass each, looking at the water, and a sixth glass is set on the cairn, poured and left standing, for the friend who did not live to be eighty. An hour later Priya arrives alone, with Arthur's next medication and a blanket, not with police: a deputy manager officially off duty, in her own words, for the rest of her life if anyone asks. Arthur is brought home safely, and two weeks later he dies at Sunnyvale, in his own bed, a person to the end, with the empty bottle on the shelf in the day room where the whole home can see it. The film's final scene is a small one. A new resident arrives at Sunnyvale, miserable and refusing everything, and there is a knock at his door. Mags stands there with two cups of tea. "We have a club," she says.

Main characters

Margaret 'Mags' Ellery

Club leader, former headmistress

Eighty-one and incapable of asking permission. She drew up the escape plan like a curriculum and runs rehearsals; the trip is also her refusal to let Arthur die as a patient instead of a person.

Arthur Bell

The dying friend

The pact was his idea in 1968 and his request now. Frailer than he admits and funnier than everyone, he is the only one allowed to say what the trip is really about.

Stanley Kowalski

Retired safe-cracker

Seventy-nine, hands shaking, memory flawless. Sunnyvale's medication safe is, in his professional opinion, an insult — and opening it without leaving a mark becomes the club's first operation.

Thirty-eight, drowning in compliance paperwork, and the club's most dangerous opponent — until she reads Arthur's file and has to decide what her job is actually for.
