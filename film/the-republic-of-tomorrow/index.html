Story Overview

Thirty years from now, the Republic governs by the Foresight Index, a predictive model of celebrated accuracy. Patrols surge where violence is forecast, before any violence has occurred. At every election, districts the Index scores as high-risk find their polling places quietly consolidated, for public safety. Crime is down. Trust is calm, actuarial, nearly total — and the calm is the achievement the Republic is proudest of, the proof it offers any citizen who asks why a prediction should be allowed to arrange a polling hall, a patrol route or a life.

Election auditor Ingrid Halvorsen, forty-five, has certified that system for a decade, and she believes in it the way actuaries believe in tables. Her belief is not laziness. She has seen the before: the disordered years the older generation describes, the counts of victims that arrived too late for anyone to prevent. Certifying the Index is, to her, a form of care performed in arithmetic — unglamorous, exact, and done so that strangers will not be hurt. Her weapons are audits, appeals and arithmetic, in that order, because those are the weapons of the world she still half-believes in.

Auditing a disputed election result, she finds a sealed annex in her own file. The Index predicts, with 91 percent confidence, that she will commit a violent felony next Tuesday, at an address she has never visited, alongside a man she has never met: Tomas Reyes, a school caretaker. The state, helpfully and lawfully, introduces them. For monitoring. There is no arrest. There is something worse, and the film insists on its exact temperature: a gentle, procedural pre-management of her life, in which her clearance is temporarily narrowed, her insurer is notified and her polling place is moved — every step reasonable, reversible in principle, irreversible in fact.

Ingrid fights as an auditor fights. She appeals, and discovers an appeals process designed to be survived rather than won. She follows procedure, and finds that procedure itself has a direction. Meanwhile the prediction has given her a person: Tomas, warm, funny and quietly terrified that the forecast may know him better than he knows himself, a man whose ordinary life — his school, his family, his Tuesdays — is now evidence in a file he did not write. Those Tuesdays are his nine-year-old daughter's: she stays with him on Tuesdays under a guarded custody arrangement, and the school's safeguarding review prompted by his flag now threatens that custody directly. Their monitored meetings are the film's saddest joke and its human centre: two strangers required to meet as future accomplices, discovering that any genuine rapport between them can be read, by the system that introduced them, as corroboration.

Above Ingrid stands Minister Elena Voss, the Index's political architect and her former mentor, who does not deny the system's cruelties. She prices them, annually, against the murders that did not happen — and her arithmetic is not easy to beat. Below the Republic's polished surface, in its unrenovated blind spots, live the flagged: people the Index marked years ago, who have organised a quieter life unwatched, and who ask the question the whole film is built to carry into public. Is a person what they do, or what they were predicted to do? A political science-fiction thriller staged entirely in audits, annexes and appeal windows, about what a correct prediction costs the people it is correct about.

The Republic of Tomorrow

Development treatment — not a shooting script.

Act I — Setup

The film opens in polished calm, and the calm must be seductive before it is sinister. Thirty years from now, the Republic is rendered in flawless plazas, pre-computed light and crowds that move a fraction too smoothly — a world that looks as if it has already been predicted. An election is being certified. In a consolidated polling hall, courteous officials explain to courteous voters why their polling place has been moved this year, for public safety, and the voters, on the whole, accept it, because the Index is usually right and everyone in the hall knows the figures. Patrols surge, gently, into a district the Index has scored for the evening. Nothing happens there. In the Republic, nothing happening is the headline, published daily.

Ingrid Halvorsen, forty-five, election auditor, watches this the way its architect would wish her to: with professional pride. She has certified the Foresight Index for a decade. Her office in the Ministry of Foresight is glass, order and quiet; her citizen card, carrying her Index standing, opens every door she needs. The film gives her belief a history and a price before it gives her a file. Six years earlier she certified the consolidation of polling places across the Kestrel district, a poor, unrenovated quarter sliding toward the blind spots, together with the surge pattern that accompanied it. Among the Kestrel polling clerks pre-managed under that certification was a man named Anton Weil, a meticulous clerk of sixty whose flag rested on association scores nobody ever explained to him. His clearance was narrowed, then withdrawn; he never worked an election again, and he died, two winters later, still formally a risk. Ingrid had filed a one-line doubt against his case — the only doubt she filed in a decade — and had let the system close it without pursuing an answer. She told herself the arithmetic was larger than one clerk. Her father still lives in Kestrel, in the flat she grew up in, and now travels across the district to vote in the consolidated hall she certified. She visits him on Sundays and does not discuss the journey.

Her present task is routine: auditing a disputed result from a district where the losing side alleges the consolidation itself changed the outcome. Working through the certification chain, Ingrid requests her own auditor file — a formality, to evidence that her access was clean — and finds, attached to it, a sealed annex she has never seen. She opens it in procedural silence, at her own desk, because an auditor opens annexes. The annex is a felony forecast. With 91 percent confidence, the Index predicts that Ingrid Halvorsen will commit a violent felony next Tuesday, at the plaza address of a Foresight records annex across the city — an address she has never visited — alongside a named accomplice: Tomas Reyes, a school caretaker she has never met. There is no warrant, no charge and no arrest. There is, within the hour, a courteous notification that a pre-management protocol has been initiated, for her own protection and the public's, and an invitation to a monitored introductory meeting with her co-predicted, so that the state can observe the risk it has identified. The Index has arranged its own cast. Act I ends with Ingrid doing the most characteristic thing she will do in the film: not fleeing, not raging, but requesting, through channels, the full input record of her own forecast. She intends to clear her file by the system's rules. She still believes the rules are the point.

Act II — Escalation

Pre-management closes around her in reasonable steps, and the film varies the species of pressure so that procedure never becomes a list. Her clearance is temporarily narrowed, which quietly removes the audit she was conducting — she can no longer clear the disputed result that led her to the annex. Her insurer is notified, and her premium notice arrives rewritten in language so gentle it takes her a paragraph to understand she is being priced as a future felon. Her Ministry housing is tied to her clearance grade, so the narrowing is also, potentially, an eviction conducted entirely in footnotes. Her polling place is moved — the auditor of elections, reassigned by her own system to vote in a hall across the district, as she once reassigned Kestrel. Neighbours are reassured by a liaison visit she is not invited to attend. Each measure is lawful, documented and reversible in principle. Each is also, the film shows without underlining, a fact about her that did not exist before the forecast, and each new fact is available to the forecast.

She appeals. The appeals process is the Republic's masterpiece: courteous, thorough, staffed by people who believe in it, and designed to be survived rather than won. Appealing raises her engagement score, a metric intended to measure a flagged person's cooperation, which the Index also reads as fixation. She consults a lawyer; the consultation is logged, and the lawyer receives, within days, a courteous notification that his own file has been annotated for association — he withdraws, apologising, and she cannot honestly blame him. Her closest colleague, a junior auditor named Petra Lindqvist whom Ingrid trained, learns of a blind-spot errand Ingrid has run and reports it through the staff welfare channel, out of genuine, tearful care: Petra believes reporting is how you save a colleague from becoming an incident. There is no villain in the corridor. There is only procedure, working exactly as designed, through people who mean well.

The monitored meetings with Tomas Reyes are the act's human centre, and the film protects their warmth precisely because the system will use it. Tomas is thirty-eight, caretaker at a primary school on the edge of Kestrel — mending, unlocking, knowing every child's lost property by heart — and he lives in the caretaker's flat adjoining the school, where his nine-year-old daughter stays with him on Tuesdays and alternate weekends under a custody arrangement he guards more carefully than anything he owns. He has never been flagged before; Ingrid's annex is his first file, which means the state has handed a school caretaker a felony forecast, a monitoring order, and a safeguarding review at his school, in a single week, because a model paired him with a stranger. His first remedy is total compliance: he attends everything, signs everything, answers everything twice, believing cooperation will exhaust the suspicion. It does not, because cooperation is data too. His humour is real and so is his fear, and the two strangers, meeting under observation in a Ministry interview room with water glasses aligned like exhibits, discover an ease with each other that frightens them both — because rapport, between co-predicted persons, is corroboration. The film's saddest joke is played straight: the Index may simply be accurate about them, and neither of them will ever be able to prove it is not.

Ingrid is received, once, by Minister Elena Voss — not summoned as a suspect, but invited as a former student, which is worse. Voss built the Index out of the old statistics service, where as a young demographer she spent years counting the Republic's murders after they happened, compiling the annual tables of the dead that nobody could act on. She tells Ingrid, without self-pity, that she can still recite the worst year's figure, and that the Index exists so that no one has to compile that table again. Then she does the film's central set piece as a mentor's kindness: the arithmetic. On the Ministry's glass she walks Ingrid through the prevented murders, year by year, priced like a harvest — the assaults that did not occur in surged districts, the elections completed without violence, the actuarial value of a decade of calm — and sets against it, honestly, the cost column, in which Ingrid's own file is one line among thousands. Voss does not deny the cruelties. She prices them. Her offer, made gently and meant genuinely, is a voluntary supervision order: Ingrid signs, accepts monitoring as a permanent condition, and the annex is sealed forever, her career and housing preserved inside supervision. Safety, at the price of agreeing, in writing, that she is who the model says. Ingrid does not sign. She asks, instead, for her input record — and Voss, who taught her to ask exactly that, does not refuse her.

What the input record shows, when Ingrid traces it with an auditor's patience, is the midpoint, and it is an idea with consequences rather than a twist sprung on the audience. The original signal was real, and the film is scrupulous about its reality, because Voss's argument must be beaten at its strongest or not at all. A cruder earlier model had flagged a genuine, weak pattern: in historical incident data, a small class of insider-facilitated offences at records facilities shared faint features — an auditor querying certification data outside her usual pattern, as Ingrid had legitimately done on the disputed result; a facilities worker whose Tuesday rounds passed the annex's service edge, as Tomas's school stores run, on Tuesdays, legitimately did. On those features alone, no honest model would have spoken above a modest confidence, and none did. The 91 percent was built afterwards, by the Republic itself: the sealed annex triggered monitoring, the monitoring generated association data between two people who had never met, the pre-management generated engagement, exclusion and grievance markers, and the Index, ingesting the state's response as if it were the citizens' behaviour, cited its own manufacture as corroboration. It is not observing her future. It is manufacturing it, then scoring the manufacture as accuracy. The horror the film has been building is now explicit, and it must be stated plainly because it is the film's thesis: nobody hacked the Index, nobody edited it, nobody invaded it. It is working as designed. In the same days, she watches the loop run faster on a stranger: a young flagged courier in Kestrel panics under a surge stop and runs, and his running becomes, within hours, the justification for everything done to make him run.

Drawn by that understanding, she descends into the blind spots: the unrenovated districts the Index sees poorly, where the flagged underground lives off-grid in grain and shadow — the film's second visual grade, deliberately rougher than the Republic's polish. There she meets Old Marek, fifty-nine, flagged at nineteen for a violent crime scheduled forty years ago that has never occurred, who has lived unwatched ever since, committing nothing, forgiven nothing. Marek's underground is not an army: it is a mutual-aid network of the predicted — couriers, safe addresses, people who will witness for each other — and it disagrees with itself, some younger members arguing that only sabotage will ever be believed, an argument Marek forbids absolutely, because any violence by the flagged is the Index's best recruiting document. What Marek wants, after forty years, is not revenge or even vindication. He wants witnesses: one public occasion on which the flagged are seen doing nothing. He wants Ingrid's case as that occasion, and is honest that he wants it. Helping the underground, Ingrid notes, would make her precisely the conspirator the forecast describes. She goes home unpersuaded and unable to stop thinking — which, her file would say, is engagement.

Act III — Resolution

Tuesday is forty-eight hours away when Voss makes the final offer in person, in Ingrid's own flat, without officials, as one professional to another. Disappear quietly into supervision, Voss says, and live — housed, employed, monitored, sealed. Or refuse, and be made the Index's public proof: the state will be waiting at the predicted address, and any incident there, however it arises, will close her file as a successful forecast. Marek, the same night, urges the opposite disappearance: vanish into the blind spots tonight, as he did at nineteen, and live. Forty years, unwatched. Free, and erased. Ingrid refuses both, and her refusal is an auditor's, not a rebel's. She uses the one channel the Republic cannot quietly close: the statutory public audit record. Through her remaining auditor credentials, in the ordinary disclosure procedure she has used for a decade, she places on the public record a formal audit observation — that she will attend, in person, at the predicted address, at the predicted hour, accompanied by her named co-predicted, unarmed and in the open, to observe and record whether a felony occurs. The announcement is lawful precisely because it is hers, filed correctly, in daylight, through the system she certified. The Republic cannot pre-disperse a registered audit observation without conceding, in writing, that its forecast is a punishment in search of an offence. Marek's network does the rest, carrying word through the blind spots by hand, the way the flagged have always carried everything the Index must not see being carried.

On Tuesday, Ingrid and Tomas walk to the records annex plaza together, in the open, in daylight. She carries a book, as she announced. He has arranged for his daughter to be with her mother, and he is more frightened of losing his Tuesdays than of the plaza, and says so, once, quietly, which is the bravest line he is given. They stand at the predicted address, at the predicted hour, the predicted two people, unarmed, reading — and they are not alone. Marek has come out of the blind spots for the first time in forty years, blinking in the Republic's polished light, a man spending his whole hidden life on one public hour, and with him, arriving singly and in pairs, holding their sealed annexes, are hundreds of the flagged. No speeches. No banners the Index can quote. Complete silence, in daylight, committing nothing. The predicted hour arrives. It passes. No crime occurs. The forecast fails, publicly, at 91 percent confidence, in front of everyone the Republic taught to trust it.

The consequences are the film's coldest passage, and the film refuses rescue. By evening bulletin, the gathering itself is classified as the predicted felony — an unlawful seizure of public space — and the arrests are gentle, lawful and total: courteous officers, documented procedure, no raised voices. The Index closes Ingrid's file as a successful interdiction; her innocence is reclassified as the offence, and the paperwork is impeccable. Voss, watching the bulletin alone, understands exactly what has been spent to protect her arithmetic — the film gives her one silent beat, and no absolution — and signs the closure, because the alternative is to compile the old table of the dead again. Tomas is released without charge. Days later, at the school gate, his phone lights with a courteous notification: a new date has been added to his file. The Republic is intact. But the flagged have stood in front of a prediction, together, in daylight, doing nothing — and been seen doing it, by each other. The final image holds on the plaza after the arrests, empty, polished, pre-computed light falling exactly where it was forecast to fall, on no one — while, off-screen in the blind spots' grain, a hand copies the date, the hour and the count of those who stood into a paper ledger the Index will never ingest.
