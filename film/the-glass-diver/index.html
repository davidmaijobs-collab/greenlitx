The Glass Diver

The station has been expecting its diver.

Salvage diver Rita Duarte is hired to strip a decommissioned research station three hundred meters down — and finds it lit, pressurized, growing food, and keeping a meticulous log of dives she has not made yet, in her own handwriting.

Story Overview

Rita Duarte takes the jobs other divers refuse, and she takes them for a reason she keeps as tidy as her equipment checks. An unauthorised recovery dive left her carrying a €58,000 insurer's settlement: she disobeyed a recall to bring up a data recorder that cleared a dead partner's name, and let an expensive rented vehicle go instead. The Aegir fee, with its completion payment, would clear what she owes and quiet her reputation. Her creed has carried her this far. Strip it, log it, leave. Do not fall in love with a wreck. Do not stay a minute longer than the work requires.

Aegir Station gives her no chance to keep that creed. The habitat was abandoned in 1998, when its funding and its chief scientist, Dr. Halvard Nyström, vanished in the same quarter. It should be dark, flooded and broken by pressure. Approaching it, Rita watches its lights come on in sequence along her path. Inside, the air is breathable, the pressure is welcoming, and gardens are still growing behind glass. On a desk that should have rotted sits a paper logbook, dry in a drowned world, open at the day of her arrival and written in a hand she knows better than any other: her own.

The salvage list becomes an investigation, because Aegir answers every act of work. Rita is there to remove valuable instruments in a fixed order, seal what remains and leave the hull ready for demolition. Yet each removal changes the place around her. Systems fail or reroute. Growing lights alter. A station that has supposedly been empty for twenty-six years behaves less like a ruin than like a household disturbed by the person sent to empty it. The deeper she works, the harder it becomes to tell which damage she is causing, which she is discovering, and which the station seems to pay to make her stop and look.

Nyström is still there in the only way he can be: in recordings. His tapes do not agree with each other. One voice urges whoever finds them to leave at once. Another asks the listener to stay and hear what the station has been hearing. Rita has to judge a man she cannot question from the evidence of his own changing words, and decide whether the recordings are a diary, a warning or bait. Her method is the method of her working life: check the physical evidence, distrust the tidy version, and never let a voice on a radio do her looking for her.

Above her, the human pressure mounts in a kinder voice. Operations chief Marcus Feld first talks about schedules, then about money, then about windows that cannot be moved. Every call is reasonable. Every call leaves Rita less room than the one before. ROV pilot Anouk Berg, who has worked beside Rita before and knows her paper dive logs, her shorthand and her habits, is the one person on the surface ship who treats what the cameras show as real. Keeping Rita's feed open becomes its own contest, fought in a control van while the demolition clock runs and the company that hired them both decides what version it can afford to have witnessed.

Under it all, the station's hydrophones carry a rhythm from the trench below, a pattern that has answered Aegir's signals for twenty-six years and whose intervals shortened dramatically on the morning Rita arrived. It is an underwater adventure built around one embodied performance: a woman alone in a suit, in a hull and in black water, doing skilled physical work while a mystery changes under her hands. It asks what she owes a place that appears to have been waiting for her, what the truth is worth against the fee that would free her, and who gets to write the record of a diver if she does not come back.

The story

Salvage diver Rita Duarte is hired to strip Aegir Station, a habitat abandoned three hundred metres down in the Norwegian Sea, before demolition charges finish it. She expects a flooded tomb. Instead she finds the lights rising for her, gardens growing behind glass, the air at a welcoming surface pressure, and a dry logbook that records her arrival in her own handwriting. Above her, Marcus Feld is drafting the report in which she never surfaces. Only ROV pilot Anouk Berg believes what the cameras show.

The Glass Diver

Development treatment — not a shooting script.

Act I — Setup

The film opens in black water, on sound before image: a transfer pod's hull ticking as it descends, Rita Duarte's breathing steady inside it, and far below, lights waking one by one along a seabed approach. Rita is thirty-nine, a Portuguese ex-navy commercial salvage diver, and the descent is shown as work, not wonder. She checks every seal twice, reads every figure back to the surface, and corrects ROV pilot Anouk Berg when Anouk shortens a call sign. Anouk, twenty-six, is flying the work ROV beside the pod and is the only unguarded voice on the circuit. Operations chief Marcus Feld, on the surface ship, is warmth itself: the job is routine, the station is cold, the demolition is urgent, and nobody could do this but Rita.

Three years earlier, on an unauthorised dive, she disobeyed a recall to recover a survey vessel's data recorder, because the recorder proved her dead dive partner had not caused the sinking that killed him. She brought the truth up and let the company's rented vehicle go. The insurer's settlement left her personally owing €58,000, payable at the end of this season, and a reputation as a diver who will break a plan for a point of honour. The Aegir contract, base fee plus completion payment for the instrument list, would clear the settlement exactly. Her creed, spoken to Anouk as the pod descends, is the film in one line: strip it, log it, leave.

The dive discipline is stated once, plainly, and never broken. Aegir stands in three hundred metres of water, where the pressure outside rises by roughly one atmosphere every ten metres. The station's interior is held at one atmosphere, breathable air, and its pressure hull carries the difference. Rita never swims free at this depth and never breathes gas at ambient pressure. She travels down in a one-atmosphere transfer pod, and for exterior work she uses the station's rigid atmospheric diving suit, which also keeps her body at one atmosphere. Her clocks are therefore real but correctly named: suit power and life support on a sortie, docking and undocking time, the demolition window, and the time a pod needs to clear the hull. No decompression schedule hangs over her, because her body is never pressurised.

The pod mates with Aegir's docking collar. The mating space is drained and checked, pressures match at one atmosphere, and Rita steps dry into a station that should be a flooded tomb. Its lights rise ahead of her. A garden bay glows behind glass, green and overgrown but alive. At the far collar she notes, because noting exits is her oldest habit, the station's emergency escape pod: a one-person, one-atmosphere capsule, docked, sealed and showing a green maintained light. Anouk reads its status back, and the film files the pod as housekeeping, not promise. At the logbook desk Rita finds the station's paper log, dry beside its sealed keeper's case, open at today's date. Her name is there. Her docking time is there. The handwriting, down to her cramped figures and crossed sevens, is hers. The first loose recording she plays is a young Dr. Halvard Nyström, brisk and frightened in 1998, telling whoever finds the tape to leave the station and tell no one it is alive.

Act II — Escalation

Rita does what she always does with fear: she starts the work list. The first item is the exterior hydrophone array. In the atmospheric suit, heavy, embodied and tethered to the station's systems, she works along the hull in black water while Anouk's ROV holds a light on her hands. As the array comes free, the garden bay behind the glass dims, section by section, as if the station has spent something it cannot replace. Rita aborts the second removal and demands an explanation Feld cannot give. He pleads the schedule. Then he doubles her money. The doubled fee would clear her debt and leave her whole; it is the kindest call he has made, and the first one she does not believe at all.

Inside, the salvage reroutes station power and the sealed laboratory cycles open. It holds dive plans in Rita's hand, dated ahead, meticulous schedules for work she has not done: routes, removals, timings. Rita studies them the way she would study a forgery, because forgery is what she decides they are. Her crossed sevens, her shorthand for a completed check, even an old error in how she writes one compass point are all present. Her contractor file, transmitted to the station ahead of her pod for docking clearance, contained her signed dive plan and specimen log pages. Someone had her hand to copy, had her arrival manifest, and had prepared her work as carefully as Feld prepared his contract. The mystery changes shape under her hands: not a book that knew the future, but a desk prepared for a named successor.

The tapes give her the man, in pieces, and Rita adjudicates them rather than merely listening. A recording from the station's first years begs divers to leave before the company learns what the hydrophones heard. A later one, the voice older and angrier, begs them to stay and listen. She trusts neither on its word. She checks tape labels against medicine stores, garden growth against maintenance entries, and the station's own system stamps against what each voice claims. In the sealed sick bay she finds the physical answer: Nyström's body, days dead, with a medical log in his own hand recording his failing heart and his decision not to call for evacuation. He did not vanish in 1998. He refused to leave, was declared dead by an operator that wanted the station closed, and stayed as its keeper for twenty-six years, tending the gardens, the pressure and the log, aided by the station's automated housekeeping. The final recording, dated this week, is real: an old man, twenty-six years past his official death, recording a handover in the last days of his life.

The oldest recording is in the laboratory, and she reaches it only now because the laboratory was power-gated until the array came out. On it, the young Nyström explains the keeper's ritual for a station that must never be left to strangers. A keeper keeps the log as events are lived, prepares the desk for the next diver, and writes the newcomer's first line in the newcomer's own hand, copied from the file, as proof the file was read and the arrival was expected. Keepers write the entries as they live them; the arrival page was written that morning, as the approach sensors lived it, by a dying man who then left the book open and went to the sick bay to die. The handwriting is hers because the welcome was made for her, deliberately, even coercively. His later tapes change because he changed: first he wanted the station hidden, then he wanted its record saved, and at the end he leaves the last page blank and says, on the final tape, the one honest thing. She may refuse the desk. Nobody is to stay for him. On that same final tape he leaves the keeper's record of the rhythm as he has kept it for twenty-six years: the pattern has always been an unfinished phrase, answering and answering again, and it will end only when someone answers it completely, once, without stopping.

Above, Anouk finds the other written Rita. Auditing the job's safety file, she opens a completed incident report, timestamped before Rita's pod ever left the surface, stating that the diver failed to return and that recovery was not attempted. It is not a blank contingency form; the cause is filled in. She sends a copy down the line and then does the decisive thing: she locks Rita's live feed into the ship's independent safety recording, the one record Feld cannot edit. Feld is operations chief, not master of the vessel, and the ship's captain will not darken a live diver's feed on a company order. Anouk can be threatened, removed and ruined, but she cannot be unwitnessed after the fact. Her belief has its own evidence: she knows Rita's paper logs from two earlier jobs, recognised the hand before Rita admitted it was hers, and once, after the unauthorised dive, quietly trimmed a footage file to protect her. She will not trim anything again. Feld's counter-move is to reveal, by accident of kindness, his own stake: two years ago he certified Aegir flooded and unoccupied from a desk assessment, and that certification won his company the clearance contract. A living station does not merely embarrass him. It proves he signed what he never checked. Behind his company stands the station's former operator, paying for clearance and silence before a new seabed-cable licensing survey can reopen the file of a scientist it declared dead. Demolition is not housekeeping. It is the destruction of the evidence, on a date, for a fee.

Act III — Resolution

The demolition charges arm early. Feld calls it an accident of procedure, in the gentlest voice he has used yet, and orders Anouk's ROV to recover the instrument package and let the rest go. A first, small charge, fired to test the circuit, shudders the hull and jams the main docking collar with Rita's transfer pod still mated to it. The controlled way home is gone. What remains are choices, and Rita must rank them in the open, on a live feed. Anouk's ROV can make one useful climb clear of the blast before the full sequence fires: it can carry the instrument package Feld is paid for, or a sealed evidence canister, or it can spend the same time attaching a guide line that would steady the station's old escape pod on its one ascent. It cannot do two of those things.

Rita sends the evidence up. Into the canister go the station's hard drives and Rita's complete scans of the logbook pages; the physical book itself she closes into its sealed keeper's case and latches the case in the desk rack where a recovery team will one day find it. The instruments, and the completion payment, and the clear run at her debt, stay on their mounts. Before she leaves the desk she writes the keeper's entry for the day herself, in her own true hand, and makes it a vow rather than a report: she came down, she was expected, she is going up. It is the first entry in the book that no one else has written for her.

She stays for one full exchange, because Nyström's final tape, the drives and the shortening rhythm have together taught her what the station has been doing for twenty-six years. The pattern from the trench is not a voice and is never given a shape. It is a rhythm that has answered the station's signals at lengthening cost and shortening intervals, and in the station's last hours Rita understands, from the keeper's own records, that the intervals have been running down toward the station's final day. She sits at the console, in the failing light, and transmits the keeper's answering pattern once, completely, without stopping when the hull begins to fail. The trench answers. The rhythm completes, in sound only, and then, for the first time in twenty-six years of recordings, it does not begin again. Whether she has closed a signal, kept an appointment or simply been heard, the film does not presume to say. She has been the witness the station kept a light on for, and she leaves before witness becomes a reason to stay and die at a desk.

The escape pod is where Act I left it, because she checked it the way she checks everything. Rita enters it dry, still at one atmosphere, seals it herself and works the manual release the keeper maintained. Without a guide line the ascent is rough and blind: the pod scrapes clear of the collar, the demolition fires below it as it rises, and the shock rolls it once before its buoyancy steadies. She surfaces alive into daylight, a ship's boat and Anouk's still-open feed, with no pressure illness to pay, because her body was never pressurised, only exhaustion, ringing ears and hands that will not obey her. Evidence and diver have surfaced by different routes, and neither of them is the version Feld wrote.

On the surface ship the record does its slow work. The captain confines Feld to his cabin and hands the timestamped incident report, the locked feed and Rita's canister to the investigators who meet the vessel; no verdict is claimed, only that the paperwork now has witnesses. Feld's company offers Rita a settlement that would clear her €58,000 in full if she will sign a statement that the station was empty and unpowered. She refuses it in Anouk's hearing. The debt therefore stands, the completion payment is gone, and Anouk loses her company job for the locked feed, exactly as she knew she might. What they have instead of money is the record, intact and jointly held. Years later, a recovery operation Anouk supervises raises the sealed keeper's case from the wreck. Rita, older, ashore and still paying her life back, watches the case opened on the recovery deck. The logbook inside is dry. Its last page is in her hand, and the film holds on the only line of it she ever wrote there, a vow the surface has kept: she came up, whatever they always write about her. Final image: the dry page, Rita's hand resting beside it without taking the book, and behind her, unremarkably and at last, open sea in daylight.

Main characters

Rita Duarte

Commercial salvage diver, 39

A Portuguese ex-navy diver whose depth record is unofficial because the dive was. She takes impossible jobs to stay ahead of a debt and a reputation; the station offers her the first mystery she cannot strip for parts and leave.

Dr. Halvard Nyström

Aegir's vanished chief scientist, present in recordings

Officially dead since 1998, Nyström narrates the station's logs in video recorded across decades he should not have survived. Whether the tapes are a diary, a warning or a lure is the question Rita must answer at depth, alone.

Marcus Feld

Salvage company operations chief, on the surface ship

Feld sold Rita the job as routine and the demolition as urgent, because his company is being paid for silence about what Aegir heard in the trench. His arc is told in radio calls that get kinder as his orders get worse.

Anouk Berg

ROV pilot and Rita's surface partner, 26

She flies the robots that watch Rita dive and is the only person who believes the logbook entries in real time. Her motivation is professional love: nobody's telemetry has ever looked like this, and she will burn her career to keep the feed open.

Signature Scenes

The Lights Along Her Path

Descending through black water toward a station abandoned for twenty-six years, salvage diver Rita Duarte watches its approach lights switch on one by one along her path, guiding her toward a hull that should be dark, flooded and dead.

The Salvage That Dims the Gardens

Working outside the hull in a rigid atmospheric suit, Rita removes the station's hydrophone array and watches the gardens behind the glass dim section by section, learning that every instrument she salvages costs the living station something real.

The Report Read to a Living Diver

On the surface ship, Anouk Berg finds a completed incident report describing Rita's failure to return, timestamped before the dive began, and reads it aloud over the open circuit to the living diver it was written to erase.

One Full Exchange

Alone at the keeper's console in a failing station, Rita transmits one complete answering pattern into the trench below and waits in silence as the rhythm that has answered Aegir for twenty-six years replies, in sound alone, a final time.
