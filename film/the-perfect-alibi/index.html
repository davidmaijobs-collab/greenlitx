Story Overview

Frankie Doyle, forty-nine, was once the face on Britain's favourite detective show. For nine seasons he played Inspector Ray Cole on Cole Justice: the coat, the catchphrase, the slow turn to camera a generation can still do from memory. Then the offers thinned, the repeats kept him famous in a tense he cannot spend, and Frankie became a masterclass — teaching acting in a Bristol community hall to students who grew up on him, He is vain, funny and hard to embarrass, because embarrassment requires a career left to lose. What he has is a gift he only ever got to pretend with, and one legendary scene.

The scene is his own writing: Cole, cornered, confesses to a murder he did not commit to draw the real killer into the open. For the class showcase, with twelve students filming on their phones, Frankie performs it in full: motive, method, remorse, forty minutes of a man describing a killing in the first person while his class holds its breath.

Two streets away, that same night, a property developer is found dead in circumstances the first officers recognise before they can say from where. The scene follows the episode — in one particular, a version of the episode television audiences never saw.

So Frankie acquires the perfect alibi and the perfect problem in the same evidence bag. Twelve witnesses and time-stamped footage prove he was in the hall when the murder happened. The same footage proves nobody alive could describe the crime as he describes it — because he wrote it, years ago, and then watched someone else perform his fiction with a real victim. His scripts become a blueprint; his old interviews are re-edited into something that sounds like intent.

The detective assigned is DI Sarah Okonkwo, thirty-nine, meticulous, dry, and privately compromised by her own childhood: she grew up on Cole Justice and can do the turn to camera. She also notices, before anyone else, the thing in Frankie's performance that does not fit a copycat working from the broadcast — which makes her both the best investigator he could have and the one least able to protect him, or herself. Her superiors understand the value of a celebrity arrest, cleanly closed. Sarah understands evidence. Between those understandings, a fan must arrest her hero before every news camera in Bristol, then decide what she actually believes.

Help arrives in the least trustworthy available form. Gloria Doyle played Cole's pathologist for nine seasons and was married to Frankie for most of them; she now hosts Exonerated, a true-crime podcast growing very fast on the strength of one story. Gloria's archive of the show — scripts, rehearsals, the production memory nobody else kept — may be the investigation's most valuable resource. Her help is real. So is her release form. Frankie, who needs her records, her memory and her nerve, must keep deciding whether he is her ex-husband, her subject, or her season.

Around them gathers the class: earnest, frightened, suddenly fluent in the language of evidence because their teacher taught them scenes in it. Ben Whitlock, the most gifted student Frankie has, filmed the confession from three angles and keeps producing assistance so complete it begins to look like its own kind of statement. The students form an amateur legal team, leak what they should hold and hold what they should share — while outside the hall, a real investigation must stay unperformed long enough to work.

The comedy of The Perfect Alibi is never the murder, which the film treats as a death, with a victim and consequences. The comedy is character: a man who cannot enter a room without blocking it like a set; a detective whose professional face must survive her inner twelve-year-old; an ex-wife conducting a divorce, a reunion and a broadcast in the same conversation. Under it runs a fair-play procedural in earnest. Can Frankie Doyle — only ever believed while pretending — be believed while telling the truth?

The story

Frankie Doyle played Inspector Ray Cole for nine seasons and has played nothing since. Teaching acting in a Bristol community hall, he performs the confession scene he wrote — and that night a developer is murdered two streets away, exactly as Frankie performed it, including something that was never broadcast. Twelve students filmed him, so his alibi is perfect and his problem is total: the witnesses who prove he was elsewhere also prove he authored the crime. A crime comedy about a TV detective framed by his own fiction.

Act I — Setup

Frankie Doyle, forty-nine, teaches an acting masterclass in a Bristol community hall — stackable chairs, a kettle that predates his fame, a poster of himself Blu-tacked beside the fire-exit sign. Nine seasons as Inspector Ray Cole bought him a face everyone recognises and a career nobody will underwrite: the offers are grandfathers, adverts and panel shows. The masterclass is rent, dignity and audience in one booking, and he teaches it superbly. That is the wound under the jokes: the work he is brilliant at is work he is only permitted to demonstrate, never to do.

His students treat him with the cheerful cruelty of the young, quoting catchphrases he is trying to teach them to stop needing. Ben Whitlock, twenty-six, a former call-centre worker studying at night, is the exception: genuinely gifted, watchful, broke, filming Frankie's demonstrations from three angles because, he says, one camera lies about where a scene's truth is standing. Gloria Doyle appears first as a voice — her podcast Exonerated in a student's earbud — then in person, returning a box of his memorabilia. Gloria played Dr. Ruth Marek, Cole's pathologist, for nine seasons; the marriage ended two years after the show, when, as she puts it, they discovered they had been colleagues who were excellent at exits. She kept the archive the production company binned. Frankie kept the coat.

The episode at the film's centre is the series-seven story fans call "Cole Confesses," the one script Frankie ever wrote alone. Cole, to draw out a killer who stages his crimes as other people's, confesses in the interrogation room to a staged murder in persuasive first-person detail. Woven through it was the tradecraft Frankie was proudest of — the stopped-watch trick. Cole describes killing a developer in his site office, smashing the man's watch against the desk edge, and setting its hands with the tip of a pen to ten minutes before the office wall clock, so the first account of the death would be wrong in the killer's favour. The broadcaster's lawyers cut the watch passage before transmission, judging a workable method of falsifying a time of death too instructive to air. The episode became the show's legend anyway, and the cut passage survived only in Frankie's memory, the uncut production script, and Gloria's archive.

On showcase night Frankie does not perform the broadcast version. Vain and free of lawyers, he restores his own text whole — watch passage included — and gives, by every account including the phones', the performance of his life: forty minutes, twelve students filming. Two streets away, in the site office of Saltmarsh Yard, a half-converted former print works at the centre of a bitter planning fight, developer Graham Sykes is killed that night and his office arranged around him.

Sykes, fifty-eight, is given a world before he is given a fate. The film meets him alive at a public consultation in the same hall, where his scheme promises luxury flats and a community room, and local people ask where that room's rent will come from. Present and largely unnoticed is his junior partner Neil Carver, fifty-two, handling the room's edges, who offers Frankie a perfectly recalled line of Cole dialogue as a joke between fans. The audience has met the beneficiary before it knows there is a benefit. Sykes is found the next morning. Sarah's team establishes the correspondences in sequence, staged as recognition: the office arrangement copies the episode; the smashed watch copies the passage that was never broadcast.

DI Sarah Okonkwo, thirty-nine, is assigned the case — a career case, her superior DCI Rowe tells her, if it stays simple. She declares her interest as required; Rowe, wanting the coverage as much as the clearance, keeps her on it. In interview Frankie performs the confession to demonstrate acting versus intent, and Sarah — twelve for exactly one second — supplies the next line when he pauses. Filmed by the custody camera, the moment gives Rowe a documented reason to question her judgement all case. Sarah arrests Frankie outside the hall before the summoned cameras, and the film holds the source image: Inspector Cole led out in handcuffs, doing, horribly and involuntarily, his own catchphrase face — the face he makes for applause, reading in a news photograph as the face of a man who did it.

Act II — Escalation

Frankie is bailed home, away from the hall, with an ankle tag he treats as a prop he did not agree to wear. From that confinement the case is worked, because Sarah, examining the phone footage with a fan's memory and a detective's conscience, reaches the conclusion she cannot yet afford to state: the footage clears Frankie's hands and condemns his authorship. If innocent, the killer learned the crime from a text almost nobody has read; if guilty, he committed it without leaving the filmed room. Rowe's preference for the celebrity arrest, quickly closed, now has a price attached to Sarah: every hour she spends on a twenty-year-old script's custody is an hour spent publicly un-arresting the most arrestable man in Britain. She spends them anyway: rigour is the only repair for a fandom that has already cost her judgement in public once.

Every asset Frankie owns keeps being entered as an exhibit. The class, organised by Ben into an amateur legal team of formidable confidence and variable knowledge, files, posts and livestreams its way through the case, once broadcasting a neighbour's recollection so enthusiastically that the witness withdraws it and Sarah loses a lead. Gloria turns Exonerated over to the case, her help arriving exactly as advertised — real, expert, accompanied by a release form. In her storage unit Frankie watches his younger self's uncut rehearsal tape, watch passage and all; it is the act's quietest scene, and one of its funniest, because younger Frankie keeps stopping to argue about a chair. It is also where Gloria earns her red line. When a frightened, unnamed voice leaves the podcast a message plainly from inside the case, Gloria refuses to air or trail it and takes the episode down a chart she has waited years to climb — because, she tells Frankie, some things are not content.

Frankie also damages himself, irreparably and usefully, in the source's waiting-room set piece. Left waiting with the nervous wife of a man questioned in another matter, he falls into Cole's gentle manner and draws from her, in four minutes, the admission her husband's solicitor spent a day protecting. It is brilliant, and on the station's security camera: it earns him an obstruction allegation and hands Sarah a break — the woman, calming, describes who she saw near Saltmarsh Yard's gate on showcase night — that Sarah cannot use without admitting how she got it. Gift and flaw remain the same act. At home, Frankie asks Gloria whether he did it to help or because there was a chair and a listener. Both, darling, she says. It was always both.

The midpoint is paper, not speech. In Gloria's archive Frankie finds the production notes: the network removed the watch passage on legal advice, and the uncut script was thereafter handled only by the production office, whose script coordinator logged every reader. The broadcast could not have taught it; no fan could quote what was never transmitted. The passage came to Saltmarsh Yard from a reader of the uncut script — and the coordinator's custody log, surfacing in disclosure, converts suspicion into design: the uncut script was requested and signed out three months before the showcase, by a reader with no production reason to ask for it. The frame was not opportunism dressed as homage. It was researched, prepared, and placed inside Frankie's world in advance. The log, the production office's memory and Ben's suddenly faltering helpfulness narrow the reader to one name: the class member who signed for the script, who proves to have a genuine, twelve-witness alibi for the murder night — Ben Whitlock himself.

Ben's account is the crisis the treatment promised, and it names the triangle by refusing, at first, to complete it. Ruined with shame, he tells Sarah and a silenced Frankie that he is not the killer: he is the killer's researcher. Three months earlier, broke and flattered, he was paid two thousand pounds in cash instalments by a man using a false production name, claiming an unofficial Cole Justice revival whose rights dispute required secrecy. Ben requested the uncut script, summarised the cut watch passage, reported the class timetable, and, at his payer's suggestion, proposed "Cole Confesses" as his own audition piece — which is why Frankie, flattered, chose it as the showcase centrepiece. His deliveries were left, on instructions, in sealed envelopes at the Saltmarsh Yard site office, in a pigeonhole his payer plainly controlled. The payer knew Sykes's development from inside. Ben never met him in a lit room: a voice, a false name, cash and a building. His helpfulness since the arrest was guilt conducting an investigation of its own.

From Ben's account and Sarah's paperwork, the beneficiary acquires a name and a causal chain. Neil Carver — Sykes's junior partner, the courteous man with the sign-in sheet — had the site office, the motive and the approaching ruin. Documents establish that Sykes, doubting the scheme, had secretly negotiated to sell his controlling interest to a housing association on terms that would repay the lenders and leave Carver — who had secretly guaranteed borrowing against his partnership share for a failed scheme elsewhere — with nothing but the guarantee. The sale was days from signature. Sykes dead before signature instead triggered the partnership's old buy-sell clause at a historic valuation, blocked the sale, and left Carver in sole control of Saltmarsh Yard on the eve of a planning decision that stood to make that control worth a fortune. Carver had motive, access and foreknowledge of Sykes's movements. What Sarah lacks is Carver doing anything: Ben's payer may have been any insider, and Ben's account is a chain she cannot yet take anywhere a court will follow. The machinery around Frankie does not wait: a charging decision approaches, on a theory his own footage makes horribly narratable — that he authored the design and an associate executed it. Act II ends with Sarah alone with the forensic summary, circling the one fact her team has withheld from every release, interview and broadcast: Sykes's smashed watch did not read the script's invented time. Its hands stood at 9:47 — a real time, chosen or left by the man in that office, known to the police and to no living person who was not there. Except, she realises, in a room she controls: a stage.

Act III — Resolution

The crisis is evidentiary, and the film states it without dressing it as procedure it cannot support. A correction proves nothing by itself, and Sarah will not build an arrest on theatre. What the withheld time can do — in a performance exact except for one placed error, assembled openly and on the record — is compel the man whose authorship is his vanity to choose between letting his work stand wrong and incriminating his knowledge. Frankie designs the trap, and the design is his arc completing itself: for once the performance is not his defence mechanism but a truthful instrument, built with Sarah, rehearsed under her conditions, aimed not at applause but at a single involuntary act of pride.

Gloria streams the restaged showcase from the same hall — same chairs, same scene, one chair pointedly empty: Ben, now a protected witness, is absent. Frankie performs the confession exactly as written and rehearsed, exactly as on the night — and performs the watch passage subtly, plausibly wrong. Where the script's Cole set the hands to the invented time, and where the record only Sarah's team holds says the real hands stood at 9:47, Frankie says and mimes twenty past nine, clearly, once, without emphasis, and moves on. The room notices nothing; the evening's comedy continues around the placed error.

Carver corrects it. He is watching the stream; he has attended, sent flowers, given his timeline, mourned on local radio. The correction arrives as a single message to Gloria's stream, from the account under the false production name Ben identified: not a time from any script — 9:47. No broadcast says 9:47. Ben's summary, which Sarah holds, gives the invented script time. Carver has supplied, voluntarily, on a record he knew was running, the one fact withheld precisely so that only the man who arranged that office could supply it.

Sarah does not pretend the correction alone is the case; the film's respect for its procedural engine is the point of the ending. The correction justifies bringing Carver in, and what follows is investigation, not applause: his messages, the account Ben identifies, his pigeonhole access, firm records, and material recovered through the lawful searches the breakthrough supports — among it Carver's own staging notes. Sarah makes the arrest herself, in the hall, cameras still present, in her own register: quiet, exact, no catchphrase, while Frankie, holding Cole's coat in the wings, watches the real job done by somebody real. The disposal of the case against Frankie, Carver's proceedings, and Ben's position as a cooperating witness are stated in brief closing dialogue, in terms the screenplay stage must verify.

Frankie's last scene in the hall is played to an emptying room, and it is the best work of his life. The stream ended and the chairs stacked, he stays in the cleared space and, unprompted and unfilmed, speaks the opening of Cole's confession once more, quietly, to nobody, and stops before the watch passage. He does not need it. For the first time in the film a performance ends because the truth has finished, not because the room has. The coda trades the expected comeback for the treatment's quieter one. Some weeks later Frankie is teaching again: no redemption montage, no revived series, a slightly fuller class. One chair is empty, where Ben sat, and Frankie does not fill it with a latecomer. He passes on the film's one acting note: the truth is just a performance you are not allowed to enjoy. Final image: the hall at evening, a row of phones face down at the students' own request, and Frankie Doyle, unrecorded, doing the only take of his life nobody will ever be able to play back.
