The Devil's Frequency

Don't answer after midnight

Overnight radio host Viv Cross despises her station's 'Ghost Week' stunt — until a caller named Ruthie describes the night she drowned in 1952 in details no living person could assemble, and the dead begin queueing to be heard, in the order of a single night in 1979.

Story Overview

KDXL, 1210 AM, broadcasts to truckers, insomniacs, and the lonely from a crumbling studio above a closed diner outside Bakersfield. Its overnight show, The Night Desk, belongs to Vivian "Viv" Cross, forty-seven, once a network investigative reporter, now fielding calls about chemtrails and missing time with the exhausted professionalism of someone who used to matter. A libel suit and a bottle ended the first career; six years of nights built the second from procedure. Viv screens her own calls because the station cannot afford a screener. She works a seven-second delay, keeps one hand near the dump button, and treats dead air as a professional failure. Those mechanics are her ethics made physical: nothing reaches the public unexamined, and a caller in trouble can be protected inside the delay.

Management has other uses for October. Ghost Week, a ratings stunt Viv openly despises, invites listeners to call in with hauntings and grief. On its first night, a caller named Ruthie describes the night the 1952 flood took her street: the water in the kitchen, the furniture against the door, her mother calling her name from the porch, all in period detail no living person could assemble. Ruthie Ellison drowned in 1952. She was nine. The call airs live, and by morning it is everywhere.

Viv does the thing horror is not supposed to survive: she checks. Names, dates, addresses, a mother named exactly as the records name her. Every check confirms. Then the dead keep calling, in an order Viv is the first to hear, because no one else has sat in that chair for six years: a Korean War pilot carrying another man's blame; a jazz pianist from 1963 who keeps requesting a song that was never recorded. The pianist is not really calling the living at all. He keeps asking whether "the man who listens" is still listening, and other callers fear the same presence: someone on the line who is not dead, not calling, only listening. Being heard, Viv begins to understand, is not the same as being helped, and a voice that arrives at 3 a.m., intimate as a confession, can still be asking for something.

The building remembers what the station would rather forget. In 1979, KDXL's original night host was found dead at a live board, and the night's tape was erased. Engineer Marlowe "Marl" Quinn, seventy-one, has worked at KDXL since 1974; he was in the building that night, and his long tenure is the studio's living archive. Marl maintains the analog board the way other men maintain a grave, and what he will not discuss is part of the station too.

Belief, for Viv, is not a private experience. It is a professional act with consequences, performed live. Management hears a phenomenon and sells sponsorships. Families of the dead begin calling the ordinary line, some demanding access, some demanding silence. And Denise Okafor-Pryce, thirty-nine, host of the hit debunking podcast Cold Open and once Viv's own protégée, sets out to dismantle each call as fraud, in public, with the fluency of someone who learned the trade at Viv's desk. Every night Viv keeps a caller on air she feeds that machine; every night she cuts one off, she may be silencing the only testimony the dead will ever give. Her dump button, delay, and screening questions become moral instruments, one caller at a time.

The week tightens around one question: what does a journalist owe a source who cannot consent, cannot be protected by ordinary means, and cannot call back? Viv's fact-checking keeps confirming the one story she cannot safely publish and cannot honestly kill. The Devil's Frequency is horror built from broadcast itself: delay, dead air, a held breath on an open line. Some calls you screen. Some you dump. Some you stay on, knowing exactly what staying costs.

The story

On KDXL 1210 AM, overnight host Viv Cross screens every caller herself: seven seconds of delay, one dump button, and rules she trusts more than people. She despises Ghost Week, her station's ratings stunt, until a child named Ruthie calls live and describes, in verifiable period detail, the 1952 night she drowned. Viv checks, professionally. Every check confirms. Then the dead keep calling, in order, all week, and believing them on air becomes the most dangerous thing she does.

The Devil's Frequency

Development treatment — not a shooting script.

Act I — Setup

The opening image is a hand hovering over a dump button. At 2:47 a.m., in a crumbling studio above a closed diner outside Bakersfield, Vivian "Viv" Cross, forty-seven, lets a caller about missing time run to the edge of the seven-second delay, then dumps him mid-word, cleanly, and fills the dead air without a tremor: "KDXL, 1210. You're on The Night Desk." This is her diminished kingdom and her discipline. Six years ago she was a network investigative reporter. Then she named Raymond Sutter, a haulage contractor, as the man paying off safety inspectors, on the strength of a ledger page her source had fabricated; Sutter's firm collapsed before the retraction, the libel suit took the job, the settlement took the savings, and the bottle took what was left. Her alcoholism is present tense, not backstory: she does not drink on air, ever, and drinks at home with the same measurement she brings to everything, counting units as she counts delay seconds. Fact-checking is the last load-bearing wall of her self-respect, and everyone who wants to hurt her, this week, will reach for the other two facts first.

The station's engineer, Marlowe "Marl" Quinn, seventy-one, KDXL staff since 1974, keeps the analog board alive by touch and memory. He was in the building in 1979, the night the station's original night host, Elias Brandt, was found dead at a live board and the night's tape was erased; station lore treats it as a heart attack and a scandal, and Marl treats questions about it as a fuse he declines to replace. Management, chasing October ratings, imposes Ghost Week: seven nights of invited hauntings, sponsored and trailed in advance. Viv despises it on air, within the limits of employment, and works the first night as she works every night: screening each call herself, delay armed, one eye on the queue lights.

Then Ruthie calls. A child's voice, calm and specific, describing the night the 1952 flood took her street: water in the kitchen, her mother moving furniture against the door, a neighbour's radio playing through the wall. She names her mother, and gives the address, and asks Viv to tell her mother she made it to high ground. Viv's training does what her fear will not: she keeps the girl talking inside the delay, asks the screening questions a journalist asks, and cannot find the fraud. Ruthie Ellison drowned in 1952, aged nine. The call airs. By morning it is a national story, and management's stunt has become the only thing Viv has ever hosted that matters.

Act I closes on verification as dread. Viv checks the way she was trained: flood records, the Ellison family, the address, the neighbour, the song through the wall. Every check confirms, including the detail she withheld from air precisely so that confirmation would mean something. The withholding costs her: a rival outlet airs its own version first, and management demands she stop sitting on material that belongs to the show. On the second night the queue lights again, in an order nobody has chosen, and Viv understands that Ruthie was not an interruption of Ghost Week. She was its first scheduled voice, and the week has begun.

Act II — Escalation

The queue builds its pattern one worked call at a time. A Korean War pilot asks Viv to find his wingman and tell him the crash was not his fault, and here Viv makes the error her arc will have to repay. Hungry to prove the phenomenon on her terms, she keeps the pilot on the public line too long, pursuing verifiable detail past the point where he begins, unmistakably, to beg to be taken off air. She hears the begging as material and stays. The clip travels. The wingman, Sam Whitaker, ninety-one and living quietly two counties away, is doorstepped by cameras within a day, and Viv watches a stranger's grief become her comeback's publicity. From that night her practice changes: callers are screened harder, protected by the delay, dumped without hesitation when the public line endangers them, and taken off air whenever a call turns private, at cost to the show. She is learning, caller by caller, the difference between using the dead and representing them.

Denise Okafor-Pryce turns that lesson into a war. Host of the debunking podcast Cold Open, Viv's researcher on the Sutter story a career ago, Denise believes, not without evidence, that Viv let the research chain carry blame Viv's byline should have carried; Viv believes Denise sold the inside account of the collapse to a media columnist. Both are partly right, and neither has ever said so. Now Denise dismantles each call in public, fluently, reaching for the libel suit and the drinking within the first episode, and her certainty is a brand: sponsors, numbers, a book. Then a caller uses a name only her family used for her grandfather, Samuel Okafor, a radio preacher whose voice Denise knows from family tapes, and asks, simply, whether his sermons ever helped anyone. Denise recognises him mid-recording, alone, and keeps the recognition out of her next episode at a price the audience can hear without understanding: the debunking stutters, and the certainty that built her begins, audibly, to cost her.

Beneath the exploitation war, the callers keep testifying about the presence they fear: "the man who listens," not dead, not calling, only listening, as he has since 1979. Viv distributes her midpoint deduction the way a rigorous mind actually works, in pieces, none of them sufficient alone. The Pianist, a jazz musician dead since 1963, asks for a song that was never recorded and asks after the listener, as if the living were the call's occasion rather than its destination. A caller gives a detail checkable only against next week's newspaper. Marl, pressed, confirms the smallest possible thing: that the erased tape of Brandt's last night still exists, because he kept it. Viv plays only its first seconds: Brandt, unhurried, taking a call from a woman who gives her death date as next week. What Viv hears, what Marl confirms, and what she only fears are kept distinct, and the fear is enough: the voices are not simply reaching back. Something about this frequency reaches both ways.

Marl's confession, when it comes, is an engineer's, delivered over the open board he built it on. The woman on Brandt's tape is Margaret "Peg" Hollis, a night nurse and the show's most beloved regular caller, and Brandt died trying to warn her of a death she had named herself. Her husband, Henry Brandt, the host's younger brother, could not survive the silence after her. He begged Marl for a way to keep hearing her voice when it returned on the line, and Marl, twenty-five and half in love with that voice himself from the booth, built the modification: a circuit that refuses, mechanically, ever quite to hang up. The writers' rule, never stated whole on screen, is single and strict: the modification holds one call permanently open, and while a living listener holds that line, any voice that has ever entered it can speak through any receiver on the frequency, in the order the voices first entered on the night the line opened; the line does not distinguish the living from the dead, which is why a caller can arrive before her own death, and it files whoever hosts or listens at the moment of transfer, which is why staying on it is how it takes its hosts, and why every Night Desk host since, Viv included, was entered into its order by taking the chair. For forty-six years Henry, now ninety-one and dying in a care facility with a receiver he has never switched off, has held the line open for one voice. What he is listening for, at the end, is a replacement, because a held line with no holder closes forever, and Henry intends his wife's voice to outlive him. Marl has paid for his one night's work with forty-six years of nightly attendance on the thing he built, warning no host before Viv, because no queue had ever moved before. It moves now because the holder is failing. His reckoning is plain and unsparing: he erases nothing this time, surrenders the tape to Viv outright, and asks her to decide about the line with her eyes open, the one thing nobody did in 1979.

Viv spends her new knowledge as a journalist, not a believer. She finds Ruthie's errand undeliverable as given: Ruthie's mother died in 1987. So she finds the family's surviving thread, Ruthie's younger sister Carol, seventy-five, who was two years old in 1952 and remembers nothing, and, off air, without telling management, she delivers the message in a stranger's kitchen, privately, as a debt rather than content. It is the first purely human use she has made of the calls, and it costs her the exclusive management believed it owned. On the same day, Denise makes her costly public act: on Cold Open, she retracts the fraud claim, tells her audience that one caller knew what no fraud could know, and refuses, to protect her family, to say who, knowing the refusal will be read as a hoax's collapse rather than corroboration. Her sponsors pause. Her numbers fall. She and Viv, in one brief scene of two professionals in a corridor, do not reconcile; they exchange, instead, the first honest sentences of their adult lives, his name neither spoken nor needed. Act II ends with the board lighting, once, on a powered-down studio, for Viv alone: no queue, no caller ID, only an open line breathing. The hazard is no longer that she will be believed. It is that she is already, in the line's own filing, next.

Act III — Resolution

The final night of Ghost Week is produced, against management's fury, to Viv's protocol: full delay, no sponsorship reads inside the calls, the switchboard staffed by Marl with his hand, literally, on the modification he built, and Denise, of her own choice, present in the studio as a witness rather than a rival, because a corroborator who wanted her destroyed is the only kind the night cannot be accused of planting. Viv airs the 1979 tape herself, all of it, so that the story enters the public record in her custody rather than the station's: Brandt warning Peg, Peg unafraid, and the line, at the end, doing something a telephone line should not do, continuing after both voices stop. Henry Brandt is dying across town as she plays it; attendants will later confirm the receiver was on. The queue advances through its last voices in order, each one shorter, the held line thinning as its holder fails, and Viv works them the way she has learned: screening, protecting, dumping one caller mid-sentence because what he was beginning to say would have named a living person's private grief on air. Belief, practised as a discipline, looks from the control room like a woman refusing, repeatedly, to exploit what only she can hear.

Then the last light on the board is hers to take. The voice that arrives is seventeen, funny, furious about a song that was on the radio, and it is Daniel Cross, her brother, dead twenty-eight years in a car she was driving at nineteen. He does not absolve her. What he gives her is the accident as he saw it: not her drunkenness, which the family story and her own punishment have polished for decades, but a deer, a wet shoulder, and her hand already turning before either of them understood. The correction is harder than forgiveness, because it leaves her nothing to pay with. Hearing the line begin to tear as Henry dies, Daniel tells her to hang up: "You're the host. End the show." Staying on the line is how the frequency takes its hosts; every instinct she owns commands her to stay. She pulls the master fader down on her brother's voice mid-word, an act of love performed as an act of murder, and the dead go quiet all at once, like a held breath let go. Marl, in the same gesture's echo, switches off the modification and then, at home before dawn, his own receiver: the first silence he has chosen in forty-six years. No replacement takes the line. That refusal, and not any exorcism, is what closes it.

The coda is ruled, deliberately, as acknowledgment rather than survival. Months later, The Night Desk is a small, honest show again: no stunt, no sponsors inside the calls, Viv sober in the unglamorous daily sense, drinking nothing and counting nothing, her authority restored at the scale she can honour. Denise's corroboration stands in the record beside the tape; Whitaker, visited privately and apologised to in person, has forgiven what the pilot never asked him to carry. In the last minute, the board lights once with no call: a piano, playing the song that was never recorded. It is not a new queue, not a summons, and not evidence that a replacement was found; it is the line's residue, a farewell echo the film grants the audience and denies its characters as proof. Viv smiles, terrified and grateful, and pots it down gently. Some frequencies you do not answer. You acknowledge them, and you keep broadcasting.

Main characters

Vivian "Viv" Cross

Overnight host, 47

Ex-investigative reporter; sardonic, meticulous, a functioning alcoholic whose fact-checking is the last load-bearing wall of her self-respect. Arc: from using the dead as a comeback to protecting them as sources — what a journalist owes the dead, and what a sister owes.

Marlowe "Marl" Quinn

Station engineer, 71

KDXL's engineer since 1974 — the only living person who was in the building the night the original host died. He knows exactly what the frequency does: he built the modification that lets it happen, in 1979, for a woman whose voice he still cannot forget.

Denise Okafor-Pryce

Debunking podcaster, 39

Host of the hit podcast Cold Open and Viv's former protégée, who publicly dismantles each call as fraud — and privately recognizes one caller as her own grandfather. Antagonist turned unwilling corroborator; the cost of a brand built on certainty.

The Callers

The dead, in order

Principally Ruthie (nine, 1952), the audience's heart; the Pianist (1963), who is not calling to speak to the living at all but to make sure someone specific is listening; and further voices the queue has not yet reached, arriving in the order of a single night.

Signature Scenes

Ruthie's Call, Inside the Delay

On Ghost Week's first night, a child who drowned in 1952 calls a live overnight show, names her mother and her street, and asks one favour, while her host sits with a hand over the dump button and cannot press it.

The Queueing Dead

Night after night, the studio switchboard lights in a sequence no engineer can explain and no host has chosen, as caller after caller takes a turn in an order that two professionals can log but cannot interrupt.

The First Seconds of the 1979 Tape

An engineer who has kept one erased night's tape for forty-six years finally plays its opening seconds for the only host he has ever warned, and a calm voice from 1979 changes what both of them think the calls are.

A Call on a Powered-Down Board

After hours, with the studio powered down and the show long over, one line on the darkened board lights for the host alone, offering no caller and no voice, only an open line that has learned where she sits.
