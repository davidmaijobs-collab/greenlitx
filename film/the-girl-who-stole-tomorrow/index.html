The Girl Who Stole Tomorrow

Every today is paid for tomorrow.

Seventeen-year-old Wren Alvarez can borrow hours from her own future to fix today — but every borrowed day is deducted, with interest, from a life she hasn't lived yet, and her future self has started leaving warnings.

Story Overview

Wren Alvarez is seventeen, fast-talking, funny in a crisis, and growing up in her grandmother's house in Albuquerque, in a kitchen that is the moral centre of her world. Inside one cabinet door, carved in her grandmother's hand, are the three laws of the family gift. The women of the Alvarez family call the gift the Tithe. A woman who carries it can pull hours out of her own future and spend them in the present. The laws are absolute: never borrow for yourself, never borrow twice in a moon, and never, ever check the balance.

Wren learned what the gift costs on the worst night of her life, when she was fifteen and her little brother Nico was hit by a car. She pulled three hours out of a future she had not lived and spent them in a hospital corridor, and Nico lived. For two years afterwards she kept the laws exactly. She also kept a secret ledger in which she writes down every hour she owes — because a debt you refuse to count is still a debt, and Wren Alvarez counts everything.

Ordinary life keeps presenting its bills. There is a scholarship audition — cello, the kind that could carry a girl from a careful Albuquerque household to a music school and a different life — and Wren wants it the way she has never let herself want anything. There is Jesse Luna, and one desert evening she is determined not to lose. And there is Dario Kim, her best friend since childhood and the only person on earth she has ever told: the one confidant who knows about the cabinet, the ledger and the night in the hospital corridor.

So Wren breaks the laws. The borrowings are small — that is what she tells the ledger, and herself. A few hours here, an evening there, against a whole lifetime: what could it matter? Then the notes begin. They arrive in her own locker, in her own handwriting, in a hand that is hers and older than hers: a warning first, specific about a borrowing she has admitted to no one. Then a receipt. The woman writing to her is herself at thirty-four, frightened, and running out of days — and she is not asking to be saved. She is issuing instructions, and they get colder as they come.

At the same time, the gift does something it has never done in any Alvarez story Wren has ever been told. Dario — no Alvarez blood, no business borrowing at all — discovers that he can pull time too. Where Wren borrows with guilt and writes it down, Dario borrows with joy: extra hours for exams, for parties, for being the rested, brilliant version of himself he is afraid of failing to be. And the Tithe collects from him in the open, in bursts, where everyone can see it happening.

Abuela Rosa Alvarez, eighty and formidable, watches all of it from her kitchen with a fear she refuses to explain. Rosa carved those laws and enforces them with love and terror in equal measure, and Rosa — who looks visibly younger than her own mother did at sixty, a fact nobody discusses — knows things about the Tithe the carved laws do not say. Her silence is protection. It is also, Wren is beginning to understand, a debt of its own.

This is a fantasy that stays home: no portals, no chosen ones — a girl, a ledger, a kitchen cabinet and a future that has started writing back. What Wren owes is not the frightening part. The frightening part is that the woman she owes it to has a face, a temper and a plan — and Wren has not yet worked out whether she is the person her older self is trying to rescue, or the thing she is trying to spend.

The story

Seventeen-year-old Wren Alvarez of Albuquerque can borrow hours from her own future. The Alvarez women call the gift the Tithe, and its laws are carved inside her grandmother's kitchen cabinet: never borrow for yourself, never borrow twice in a moon, never check the balance. She saved her little brother with three borrowed hours and kept those laws for two years — until an audition and an evening she wanted too much broke them. Now warnings are arriving in her own handwriting, from the woman she is spending.

The Girl Who Stole Tomorrow

Development treatment — not a shooting script.

Act I — Setup

The film opens at night, two years before the main action, in a hospital corridor in Albuquerque. Nico Alvarez, eight years old, has been hit by a car. Behind a curtain, surgeons are losing the race between his injuries and the hour. His sister Wren, fifteen, sits with her grandmother's hand crushing hers, and does the thing the women of her family can do and must almost never do: she pulls three hours out of her own unlived future and spends them in that corridor. The borrowing has no spectacle. The air goes thin, as if the room had been carried briefly to altitude. The fluorescent light shifts and steadies wrong. Pressure drops in everyone's ears, and nobody can say why the night suddenly contains more time than it should. Nico reaches surgery with hours to spare that the clock never showed. He lives.

Afterwards, in the family kitchen, Abuela Rosa Alvarez tells her the price plainly: three hours borrowed, three days gone from the end of Wren's life, entered in an account the family calls the Tithe. Then Rosa opens the kitchen cabinet. Carved inside the door are the three laws: never borrow for yourself; never borrow twice in a moon; never check the balance. The audience is meant to file away what Wren files away: the cabinet has a second door, kept shut, which Rosa neither opens nor explains. That night Wren begins her ledger — a school notebook recording the first entry of her life as a debtor. Three hours. For Nico. Worth it.

Two years pass inside the rules. Wren is seventeen now: fast-talking, stubborn, funny under pressure, a cellist good enough that her counsellor says scholarship in a tone reserved for weather miracles. She and Nico, now ten and gloriously ordinary, have grown up in Rosa's house since their mother died when Wren was ten; their mother carried the gift too, barely used it, and died of an ordinary illness — the family's plain grief, with no magic in it. Rosa cooks, enforces, and watches, and at the kitchen table tells the old stories. One matters: her younger sister Beatriz, dying young before good hospitals, for whom Rosa borrowed and borrowed — and who, recovering against every expectation, spent a long life giving time back in uncounted kindnesses, until Rosa's account, in her phrase, "stopped asking." The Tithe never billed Beatriz for the giving. Rosa's telling is precise about what Beatriz gave, even if Wren files it without hearing it: Beatriz never gave her a Tithe hour — she pulled no time from her own future and transferred nothing through the account; what she gave was her ordinary lived time, freely, with no ledger, no claim and no expectation of return. That, not any transfer of hours, is why Rosa's account stopped asking: the Tithe can price a borrowing, but it has no price for time given away, in whatever time it is given. A literal gift of actual hours from a carrier's own future is a different act from a lifetime of kindnesses, and Beatriz's story does not pretend she made one. Wren files the story under sentiment, not law.

Wren's world is Albuquerque daylight: school, rehearsal rooms, the desert edge, a brother who leaves her ordinary notes on the fridge. Two pressures build. The first is the audition — a competitive music scholarship, cello, that would take her into a life she has wanted too carefully ever to say its size aloud. The second is Dario Kim, her best friend since childhood and the only person she has ever told: keeper of her ordinary self, whose faith in her is the one place she need not be the responsible Alvarez. The act's question is set quietly: a girl who has organised her life around not spending her future is about to want things in the present tense.

Act II — Escalation

The audition moon breaks her. First comes a permitted borrowing, small and for someone else. Then, days later, inside the same moon, she borrows again — for the audition itself, pulling extra hours of practice and rest from her future so she can walk in rested and prepared. It works. She plays the best she has ever played. It is also the second borrowing in a moon, and part of her already knows whose benefit it was really for. Weeks later she breaks the first law outright. Jesse Luna — kind, funny, leaving at summer's end — asks for one thing: an evening, the desert light, no phones, no family emergencies. The evening is impossible on the night offered, so Wren borrows hours and makes it possible. It is a perfect evening. It is also, the ledger forces her to write, entirely for herself.

That is when the notes begin. The first is in her locker, in her own handwriting, older and in a hurry: a warning naming the Jesse evening and the exact hours she took, told to no living person except Dario — who did not write it. The second is a receipt: her borrowings, itemised, in an older woman's arithmetic. The writer is herself at thirty-four, frightened, and running out of days.

Dario's discovery detonates in the middle of this. He has been named in Wren's ledger once — as beneficiary of a borrowing she made for him, pulling hours so he could survive the worst exam week of his life as himself rather than as a wreck. One night, alone, Dario reaches for time as she has described it — and it comes. He can borrow. When Rosa finds out, her fury is instant and diagnostic, and it gives the film its held explanation: the gift is carried in Alvarez blood, and only Alvarez women can open an account — but the Tithe keeps its books by the ledger, and a name written into a borrower's ledger as a beneficiary is a name the Tithe can bill. Wren did not give Dario the gift. She gave the Tithe his name. The matrilineal law stands; debt, Rosa says, never was matrilineal. That is why the ledger is secret. That is why you tell no one.

Dario borrows the way Wren never could: with joy. Extra hours to study and somehow also to celebrate; extra hours to sleep after both. He is buying, he tells her, the version of himself he likes — the rested, quick Dario he fears lives just past his ordinary stamina, the one his parents' sacrifices deserve. And the Tithe, which settles resting accounts silently at life's far end, settles Dario's in the open, because his account never rests: an unsettled account is collected in bursts, on the body, where everyone can see. By winter there is grey at his temples. Each rescue Wren attempts costs hours from her own account, and her ledger entries begin to alternate: his name, her name, his name.

The notes escalate with the debt: a warning, a receipt, then a photograph Wren cannot explain and cannot unsee — a woman at thirty-four who looks fifty, exhausted past sleep, wearing a hospital bracelet printed with a date months ahead. Wren has assumed her older self is protecting her. At the midpoint, the coldest note of the film arrives, four words of triage: Don't save Dario. Save the date. The reversal lands completely. Future Wren is not protecting the girl. She is protecting a single day, at any cost to the girl in the present. The warnings are not a lifeline but resource management, written by a woman who has done the arithmetic and decided which of them it can afford. Wren is not the patient. She is the supply.

Act II closes on two arrivals. The first is a letter: Wren has won the scholarship. The future she has been spending suddenly has an address and a start date, and she hides the letter inside the ledger, unsure she has life enough left to accept it. The second is Rosa's fear, finally visible: recognising her own youth's pattern, Rosa begins to say something at the kitchen table — and stops, and closes the cabinet's second door with her hip as she passes, as she has for sixty years.

Act III — Resolution

Dario's account comes due in a single night. Facing the grey in his mirror and the terror that the borrowed self is the only self anyone has ever liked, he tries to borrow his way back — pulling hours not to spend but to repay, to undo, to be seventeen again by morning, hundreds of hours dragged through one night in a panic that mistakes the Tithe for a negotiation. The Tithe settles. He ages years between midnight and morning, collapses, and is carried to the hospital where this film began. In the collapse he makes the first free choice of his spiral: he begs Wren not to do the obvious thing — not to borrow for him. He has read her ledger's pattern, his name and hers alternating, and understands his catastrophe is priced to make her pay it. "Don't" is the bravest word he has said all year. It does not save him. It changes everything about how he can be saved.

Because the obvious thing is exactly what the loop predicted. To hold Dario in the present, Wren would have to borrow the largest sum of her life — and the photograph in her locker is the portrait of the woman that borrowing creates: the exhausted thirty-four-year-old, managing her younger self like a supply line, protecting one date because everything else is spent. Wren stands in the corridor where she once spent three hours like a hero, and refuses — not Dario, but the method. She goes home to Rosa and says the words that break the family's real law, the uncarved one: Tell me what the rules don't say.

Rosa opens the cabinet's second door. Inside is her own ledger, kept since she was a girl: decades of borrowing, the great debt of her sister's illness — then, page after page, no entries at all, because Beatriz lived, and gave, and the account stopped asking. Rosa explains what she could never carve as a fourth law, because carving it would kill it. The Tithe collects on borrowing. It has no price for a gift. Time given freely — no ledger, no claim, no expectation of return — is the one transaction the account cannot bill, deduct or collect; and a gift made as a strategy, Rosa warns, is a loan wearing perfume. The Tithe prices intent. That is also what the collector is, the thing Rosa met the night her own balance fell due: not a person, but the settlement itself arriving — air going thin in her kitchen, light shifting, a figure-shaped absence at her table, come to take what was owed and leaving, that once, with nothing. It is why Rosa looks younger than her mother did at sixty. Given years settle gently. Borrowed ones never do.

Then Wren does the last thing the laws forbid. She checks the balance. At the kitchen table they do the full accounting she has avoided for two years, both ledgers open: every entry, hers and Dario's, converted and totalled — and the total, though it frightens her, is not the horror. The horror is the date. An account, Rosa tells her, falls due on a day, and Wren's day is printed in her own future handwriting on a hospital bracelet in a locker photograph: the date Future Wren has been protecting is the day Wren's balance comes due — the day, in the life the warnings came from, the Tithe collects what remains of her at thirty-four, with nothing left over. Future Wren was never managing the girl for cruelty. She was trying to arrive at her collection day with one thing the loop never left her: a choice still unspent.

Wren's decisive act is not a borrowing. At the hospital, holding Dario's hand, she gives: hours from her own future — the real ones, the scholarship years — given outright, no loan, no entry, no claim, her ledger deliberately left at home. She pays his debt forward, into his life, instead of backward into her account. The Tithe, which collects only on borrowing, has no instrument for this. The air goes thin — then comes back fuller than it left, like a held breath released kindly. Dario stabilises, aged past his years, his account closed; in the weeks after, he keeps his panic-scribbled tally framed above his desk, a receipt for a life he is determined to live at its actual speed. He never borrows again. Neither will Wren — not from fear, but because she has finally seen the woman at the other end of the account as a person she is responsible to, not a resource she is entitled to.

What remains is the due date, and the film ends by arriving at it twice, intercutting the two ends of one life. At seventeen, Wren sleeps in Rosa's house, dreamlessly, for the first time in years, the ledger closed, the scholarship letter open on the desk beside her: a future, accepted, at its real price. At thirty-four, on the morning of the bracelet's date, a woman opens her eyes in a hospital room — not to a collection, but to a tray, and on the tray a last note. The handwriting is hers and not hers: Rosa's hand, written decades earlier on the night her own account was forgiven, sealed behind the second door for the next Alvarez woman whose day came due, and delivered the way everything in this account is delivered — as paper, arriving exactly where it is owed. Balance forgiven. Live it slowly. In a school locker across the city and seventeen years away, a photograph of an exhausted woman fades into an ordinary picture of a woman resting. The final image is small and sunlit: Wren, seventeen, walking her little brother to school in the Albuquerque morning, in no hurry at all — taking, for once, exactly as long as it takes.

Main characters

Wren Alvarez

Protagonist

Fast-talking, stubborn, and keeping a secret ledger of every hour she owes. Her heroism and her selfishness use exactly the same gift, which is the problem.

Abuela Rosa Alvarez

Grandmother, keeper of the rules

Eighty, formidable, and visibly younger than her own mother was at sixty — a fact nobody discusses. She enforces the Tithe's rules because she knows what the collector looks like: she has met hers.

Future Wren (the Notes)

Wren at 34, unseen antagonist-benefactor

Known only through warnings, receipts and one devastating photograph. Whether she is protecting her younger self or manipulating her is the film's central mystery.

Dario Kim

Best friend and rival borrower

The only person Wren tells, who discovers he can borrow too — and treats it like a credit card with no limit, aging weeks in a semester while Wren watches the bill come due.

Signature Scenes

Three Hours in the Corridor

Fifteen-year-old Wren Alvarez pulls three hours out of her own unlived future in a hospital corridor, buying her little brother's road to surgery with time the clock never shows and her life will quietly miss.

The Rules in the Cabinet

In the sunlit Alvarez kitchen, Wren reads the Tithe's three laws carved inside a cabinet door while her grandmother cooks — and neither of them looks at the cabinet's second door, which stays shut.

Her Own Handwriting

Between classes, Wren opens her locker and finds a warning in her own handwriting — older, hurried, and naming borrowed hours she has told to almost no one: her future has started keeping its own accounts.

The Gift Forward

Wren refuses the borrowing her whole story has predicted and instead gives Dario hours from her own future outright — and seventeen years on, a woman wakes on the day her balance falls due to find it forgiven.
