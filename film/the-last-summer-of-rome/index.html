The Last Summer of Rome

The empire ends. The road does not.

In 476 AD, an imperial courier rides the last dispatch of a dying empire across a collapsing Italy — carrying an order that could save Ravenna, start a war, or free him from Rome forever.

Story Overview

In the summer of 476 AD, Italy is still governed, after a fashion, from Ravenna. But the Western Empire issuing the paperwork has shrunk to a court, a boy emperor, his father's army, and the roads. What holds the fiction together is movement: orders riding out, reports riding back, relay stations changing tired horses for fresh ones, seals checked by men who have checked them all their lives. An empire, in this film, is not a map. It is a delivery system that has not yet admitted it has nothing left to deliver.

Flavius Corvus, twenty-nine, is that system's smallest indispensable man. His family has carried other men's orders for three generations: his grandfather rode for emperors whose names are still carved in stone, his father died on the Ravenna road with a dispatch in his satchel, and neither man was ever granted the Roman citizenship he served. Corvus is half Roman, half Goth, and wholly of the road. His pride is professional and exact. He does not read what he carries. He does not ask what it will do when it arrives. The road is delivered, whatever it carries, and for years that rule has spared him every harder question.

The order he is given this summer allows him no such shelter. Sealed in a palace already being packed around him, it is the court's last great gesture toward rescue, and every power in Italy understands what its arrival, its delay, or its disappearance would mean. A courier who is normally beneath notice becomes, for one season, the most courted man in the peninsula. Men who would not share a table with him are suddenly anxious to share his road.

One of them does not need to catch him. Placidia rides a parallel route with a dispatch of her own, issued in another name and ordering the opposite course. She and Corvus were lovers once, in the posting houses and short summers between commissions, and they still speak the road's private shorthand: distances in hours, weather in horseflesh, danger in what a station keeper does not say. Now each must weigh the other as colleague, rival, or something worse, because the two orders cannot both be honest. One of the sealed letters travelling toward Ravenna's fate is a forgery, and until somebody judges the paper instead of merely carrying it, neither rider can know whether they are serving their city or being used to dismantle it.

Ahead of both riders moves Odoacer, the foederati commander whose revolt has already decided most of Italy's future. In every other telling he is the man who ended Rome. In person he is patient, literate, and disconcertingly reasonable, and he makes Corvus the offer Rome withheld for three generations: citizenship, and land to go with it. His argument is as dangerous as his army. Why bleed for a court that would not make your grandfather a citizen?

In Mediolanum, Bishop Ambrosius — a later bishop who took the great Ambrose's name at his consecration — is preparing for the same ending from the opposite direction. Gentle, funny, and utterly unsentimental about the future, he is building the Church into the institution that will still be feeding, judging, and corresponding with Italy when the palace is empty, and he collects useful people the way others collect relics. His protection is real, his hospitality is real, and both come at a price he names with a smile.

Between Ravenna, the burned crossings of the Po, the quarantined villages, and an army that exists partly on paper and partly, lethally, in fact, the film asks its question at walking pace, in mud and hunger and paperwork. What is a message worth when the world that sent it is already gone? And what does the man who carries it owe: the seal he swore to deliver intact, the people its contents will fall on, or the truth as he has come to know it on the road?

The story

In the summer of 476, the Roman Empire in the West survives as paperwork, and Flavius Corvus carries it. A courier whose family served three generations without citizenship, he rides from Ravenna with the emperor's sealed order, while Placidia, his former lover, rides a parallel route with a contradictory one. One of the two orders is a forgery. Courted by the commander closing on the city and by a bishop building whatever comes next, Corvus must decide whether a courier is a man, or only an envelope.

The Last Summer of Rome

Development treatment — not a shooting script.

Act I — Setup

Ravenna, high summer, 476. The imperial court is packing as it performs permanence. In the throne room, clerks heat wax and set the imperial seal to a recall order summoning the eastern legions to Ravenna's defence, while behind them, in the same routine, servants unpick the palace hangings, roll carpets, and crate plate. On the throne sits Romulus, a boy in his teens, emperor for less than a year, placed there by his father Orestes, the general who actually rules. The East has never recognised the boy. In Dalmatia the deposed Julius Nepos still claims the title. Nobody says it. Saying things is not what seals are for.

The courier waiting in the anteroom is Flavius Corvus, twenty-nine. The Corvi have ridden the imperial post for three generations. His father died of a fever in a posting house with a dispatch under his head, and the service buried him with honours and without citizenship, as it had buried his father before him. Corvus wears his father's bronze courier warrant at his belt, the token of his trade, and keeps in his satchel, folded to softness, the family's third unanswered petition for citizenship. His creed is the family's survival strategy: he does not read what he carries, he does not judge it, he delivers. The one delivery that tests the creed is three years behind him. In the winter of 473 he carried a sealed order withdrawing the garrison from a pass fort above the Ligurian coast, rode on without a backward look, and learned a month later that the valley below the empty fort had been raided within the week.

The sealing done, a dying senior clerk, who has drafted the court's correspondence for forty years, draws Corvus aside. A first appeal, he whispers, was sent east in the spring. Constantinople answered it. The answer was no. The sealed order on Corvus's breast is a ritual, performed so the court can say, at the end, that it asked. Corvus rides out at evening through streets where the departure crowd is thin and mostly curious, past stations where the registers are immaculate and the stables half empty.

At a flooded posting house on the second night, where the road runs on a causeway through drowned fields, he finds the one person who can make the mission personal. Sister Placidia, thirty-one, is riding the opposite errand. Once the two best riders on the northern routes, and once lovers, they fall at once into the road's shorthand: horse, kit, seal, temper. Placidia is a courier of the Ravenna relay hospice, a church sisterhood that feeds, horses, and buries the road's riders, and she rides this summer under a Senate commission: a dispatch ordering Ravenna's garrison to stand down and open its gates. It contradicts the recall in Corvus's satchel in every particular. Both letters are sealed, both riders professionals; neither can open the other's order to compare, and both understand, by the fire, with the courtesies intact, what the arithmetic means. One of the two orders is a forgery. If hers is false, she is a dupe or a spy at the heart of a surrender. If his is false, his whole ride is a fraud performed in wax. They part before dawn on separate roads, promising nothing, which on the road is a kind of promise.

Act II — Escalation

The Po crossing is burned, its timbers still smoking, and the ferry replacing it is held by armed men who have invented the road's new tariff: a rider pays in either seal or blood. Corvus pays, with his father's bronze courier warrant, the instrument of his whole working life. It is the summer's irreversible transaction. From the north bank onward, Flavius Corvus has no entitlement to relay horses, food, or lodging anywhere in Italy. Nothing he loses later costs him as steadily as this: every choice in the last act is made at walking pace, while armies move at theirs.

The road itself is quarantined next. A plague village bars its own street with carts and a priest, and the choice is stated without melodrama: ride through the sick or take the hill detour and lose four days the mission does not have. Corvus takes the detour, and it costs him more than time. At the village's edge a dying relay keeper, an old friend of his father's, begs him to carry one thing onward: not a dispatch, a message, spoken, for the widow who keeps the next hospice, telling her the keeper is dead and the house's accounts are honest. Corvus carries the words anyway, learns them by heart like a seal he must not break, and delivers them aloud at the next station. It is his first small disobedience: he has delivered a true thing, unsealed, at no one's orders, and the sky has not fallen.

In the high country his errand turns on him. Gothic scouts ranging ahead of Odoacer's advance shoot his horse from a ridge, his own people mistaking him at distance for what his warrant used to say he was, and he continues on foot, leading a pack mule taken from an abandoned farm: a half-Goth courier of Rome reduced to the speed of the empire itself.

Odoacer is about forty-three, a commander of the foederati, the allied peoples, Heruli, Sciri, Rugii, and others, who rose this summer when Orestes refused them the land settlement they had been promised, a third of Italy's soil for the men who defended it. He receives Corvus not as a prisoner but as a colleague, gives him water before argument, and speaks to him as no Roman official ever has: as an equal with a grievance the commander shares. His men were promised land and refused it by the father of the boy on the throne. He shows Corvus the evidence. First, Constantinople's answer to the spring appeal: the eastern legions are not coming, and never were. Second, on the camp table, the Senate's seal matrix, stolen a year ago: Placidia's stand-down order is the forgery. Odoacer no longer needs it, and offers to burn the matrix in the fire between them, that night, if Corvus will simply stop riding.

The generosity is strategy, and the film lets Corvus see it whole. Odoacer needs Ravenna bloodless. A city talked open by the truth, carried by the empire's own couriers, keeps its Senate cooperative, its granaries intact, and his kingship presentable in Constantinople. And he wants Corvus himself, the best rider in Italy, as chief of a royal courier service still needed the morning after empires end. If Corvus refuses and rides on, Odoacer loses that clean timetable: he must advance on schedule anyway, and every day the forged order circulates is a day the garrison may act on it, fracture, and force the fighting he is trying not to buy. The closing offer is personal, and made without theatre: citizenship, under the new settlement, and land with it. Everything Corvus's grandfather, father, and petitions asked Rome for, offered by the man Rome calls its killer, in exchange for doing nothing at all. Corvus does not accept. He does not yet refuse. He rides out with the truth as his new, unpaid cargo.

On the road north he learns the summer's last arrangement of the board. The vanguard screen ahead of Odoacer's advance is commanded on its flank by an officer the scouts name with respect: Lucius Corvus, called Lupus, twenty-four, Flavius's younger brother. The brothers meet once, briefly, at a requisitioned granary neither can linger in, and it is the film's quietest confrontation: no treason in the room, only two professional inheritances. Lupus does not apologise for choosing the side that feeds, pays, and promotes him, and Flavius cannot call him wrong in any term his own creed supplies. Lupus warns him, as a brother, in the family's only real language, off the record: the vanguard has its orders about riders.

Unable to trust or abandon each other, Corvus and Placidia now ride together into Mediolanum, where Bishop Ambrosius has offered both riders the Church's sanctuary. Ambrosius is fifty-eight, a former imperial notary. He bears his famous name by his own choice: at his consecration he took the name Ambrosius in honour of the great Ambrose of Milan, dead these eighty years. Gentle, funny, and utterly ruthless about the future, he is building the Church into the institution that will outlive the empire, with granaries, hospices, and a correspondence network of his own. His offer is sanctuary, horses, and protection, at a price he names with a smile over dinner: both letters. It also frightens him, and the film gives him one unguarded moment in which to know it: Ambrosius admits to Placidia that he can no longer always tell whether he is sheltering the road or acquiring it. Neither rider surrenders a letter.

Act III — Resolution

Odoacer advances on Ravenna, and the crisis strips the question to its frame. A copy of the stand-down order has gone ahead of Placidia into Ravenna, ahead of any proof that it is forged. Placidia's response defines her, and it is not obedience in a new direction. She rides for Ravenna to expose the forgery herself, in person, before the officers who received the copy. Corvus rides with her as far as the marsh causeway below the city, where the vanguard screen is posted to stop any rider reaching Ravenna, and where its flank officer is his brother. Lupus, his troop at his back and his orders in his hand, looks at the two exhausted riders his orders describe, and does the one thing his service cannot forgive and his family would: he reports, in writing, that the causeway was found impassable and the patrol withdrawn to high ground, and lets them through, buying their passage with his own record. When the land settlement comes, Lupus takes the family's share, good acres near Placentia, granted in his name by the new order he served. The brothers part as citizens of different settlements of the same summer, neither converted, neither disowned.

Corvus enters Ravenna ahead of the army, the recall order still sealed in his satchel and Placidia's forgery beside it. Brought before Romulus, he arrives at the act the whole road has rehearsed. He delivers neither dispatch. He kneels, lays both sealed letters on the floor unopened, and reports aloud, unsealed, what no letter could carry: that no legions are coming and never were, that the Senate's order is forged and its stolen matrix lies on an enemy's camp table, that the city cannot be held, and that its emperor's last useful act is to end the matter himself, cleanly, before his people pay for a ceremony of resistance. The boy listens, asks two questions, a clerk's questions, about his household's safety, and then performs the last imperial act available to him. He abdicates. On the fourth of September, in Ravenna, without a sack or a siege, the western imperial office ends not with a battle but with a boy handing over its insignia, and being promised in return his life, an annual pension of six thousand solidi, and retirement to Campania: promises the new king keeps.

The regalia itself becomes the empire's final dispatch. The diadem, the purple cloak, and the insignia of the western office are packed in a courier's case, addressed, in effect, to the Emperor Zeno in Constantinople, with the Senate's declaration that the Roman world requires henceforth a single emperor. It is the one message of the summer whose contents Corvus is permitted to know entire, and he volunteers to carry it east. He rides because the road is his, because somebody has to carry the message, and because this one, at last, is true.

Placidia reaches the city by the marsh road ahead of him and burns her forged order in the palace brazier herself, rather than let fraud do what fact must do. Then Ambrosius's offer matures, and the film shows its terms being argued, not merely accepted. Placidia takes the bishop's commission to found the Church's courier network, but on conditions she dictates and he, smiling, accepts in writing: the network carries no sealed order its rider believes forged, its riders are paid from church granaries rather than in promises, and its first loyalty is to the road and the people on it, before any master who uses it. Asked in the palace yard whether that is not a new envelope with a cross on it, she gives the film's answer for her, distinct from his: a master you can argue with, in writing, whose terms you set, is not the same as a seal you swore never to question. Whether the institution will honour her conditions for a generation, let alone the thousand years Ambrosius cheerfully predicts, the film leaves honestly open. She has not been absorbed. She has been answered, and she stays to hold the answer to its terms.

Final shot: dawn, the road east out of Ravenna, the marshes smoking with mist. A new seal, a new addressee, the same road. Flavius Corvus rides out at a courier's working trot with the regalia case strapped where his satchel has always ridden. Roads outlive empires. Somebody has to carry the message, and for the first time in three generations of his family, the man carrying it knows exactly what it says, and chose to carry it anyway.

Main characters

Flavius Corvus

Imperial courier, protagonist

Half-Roman, half-Goth, wholly of the road. He has delivered orders that began wars and ended them, and his crisis is discovering he finally cares what this one says.

Odoacer

Gothic commander

History's footnote as empire-killer, here a patient, literate statesman offering Corvus everything Rome withheld. His argument — the empire is already dead, stop bleeding for the corpse — is dangerously good.

Sister Placidia

Imperial courier and Corvus's former lover

Riding a parallel route with a contradictory dispatch from the Senate. One of the two orders is a forgery; each rider must decide if the other is colleague, rival, or executioner.

Bishop Ambrosius of Mediolanum

Church power broker

Gentle, funny, and utterly ruthless about the future: he is building the institution that will outlive Rome, and he collects useful men the way others collect relics.

Signature Scenes

The Sealing and the Stripping

In Ravenna's throne room the emperor's last recall is sealed with full ceremony while, behind the clerks, servants strip the palace hangings and crate the plate, and a dying clerk whispers to courier Flavius Corvus that Constantinople has already answered, and refused.

Seal or Blood at the Po Ferry

At the burned Po crossing, ferrymen demand payment in either seal or blood, and Flavius Corvus buys his passage with his father's bronze courier warrant, surrendering his right to fresh horses, food, and lodging at every relay station in Italy.

The Parley of Evidence

At a camp table set like a chancery, Odoacer shows Corvus Constantinople's refusal and the Senate's stolen seal matrix, proves the parallel dispatch a forgery, and offers to burn the matrix, and grant Corvus citizenship and land, if he will simply stop riding.

The Unsealed Report

Kneeling in Ravenna's throne room, Corvus lays both sealed dispatches unopened on the floor and reports the truth aloud instead, and by dawn he rides east carrying the imperial regalia itself, the empire's final dispatch, addressed to Constantinople.
