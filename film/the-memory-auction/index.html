The Memory Auction

Your past is already sold.

When Vesper Exchange cataloguer June Park screens a routine lot and finds her own seventh birthday recorded through her mother's eyes, she discovers her entire childhood was sold in 41 lots — and the night her mother disappeared was never sold at all, but deposited in a sealed trust.

Story Overview

In 2041, memory is a luxury good. At the Vesper Exchange, the wealthy legally buy other people's lived experience — a first kiss, a summit at dawn, a wedding morning — graded and sold in velvet auction rooms like art with a heartbeat. The trade is respectable, regulated in appearance, and built on a simple cruelty: sellers are rarely people who can afford to buy.

June Park, 34, is a senior Exchange cataloguer. She grades other people's lives all day and asks nothing about her own. Her childhood is a blank with an official explanation: an accident, a therapeutic wipe, nothing left to recover. June has built a small, precise adult life on not asking — the one subject her professional curiosity never touches.

Then a routine lot reaches her desk: lot 1147, a seventh birthday, candles, a woman singing. June recognises the perspective with her body before her mind can argue. She is inside her mother's eyes, looking down at herself. The provenance line names the seller — the guardian who raised her — and the date sits three months after the accident that supposedly took everything at once. June was not wiped in a single tragedy. She was sold, piece by piece, in forty-one separate lots.

She does not report it. She buys it — with money she does not have, from the house she works for. That purchase teaches her how the Exchange holds its own: every lot she wins becomes collateral against the ones she still needs, and the employee quietly becomes an asset. To recover the rest she must bid, borrow, steal and trade her way up a buyer chain — and every recovered memory rewrites the last one. Her mother is tender in one lot, frightened in the next, secretive in a third.

The buyers are the obstacle no shortcut can remove. A widower bought June's childhood summers to live inside a family warmth that steadied him after his own daughter died; for him those summers are load-bearing, and handing them back would be a kind of killing. A rival house prices June's desperation expertly. Collectors who never met her know her mother's laugh better than she does.

Her guide through that market is Elias Vane, once the Exchange's most gifted retrieval specialist, now an eraser for hire who helps the desperate forget what was taken from them — or what they bought. He takes June's money, answers a half-beat too precisely, and knows her case in ways a hired guide should not. He was there, in some capacity, on the night her mother disappeared. He will not say in what capacity, and declines, politely, to clear himself.

At home, the wound has a second keeper. Theo Park remembers everything June lost. He kept every birthday she cannot recall and built a bitter life around the lightness of her forgetting. The Exchange wants his memories more than almost anyone's — he is its most wanted holdout — and he has refused to sell a single one. He refuses June, too: she must recover without the one living archive who shares her past, and every lot she wins lands on him as fresh proof that the past is easier for the sister who got to lose it.

Above the grading floor sits Odette Vesper, serene and entirely sincere, who founded the Exchange on one conviction: selling memory is mercy, the one asset the poor own that the rich cannot manufacture. Odette does not behave like a woman hiding a secret. She behaves like a creditor who has waited a long time for a debtor to walk in. One lot has never been listed or offered to any buyer — the night her mother disappeared. June wants it more than all the others combined, and the Exchange will protect its stock and archive long before it lets one cataloguer remember everything.

The story

In 2041, the rich do not collect art. They collect other people's first kisses, summits and wedding mornings, legally, at the Vesper Exchange. June Park, 34, grades those lots for a living and believes her own childhood was wiped in an accident — until a routine screening plays her seventh birthday back through her mother's eyes. Her past was not lost. It was sold, in forty-one lots, by the guardian who raised her. One night was never listed at all.

The Memory Auction

Development treatment — not a shooting script.

Act I — Setup

2041. The Vesper Exchange occupies a quiet, beautiful building where memory is traded the way art once was: provenance first, emotion second, price last. On the grading floor, cataloguers screen first-person lots — a first kiss, a summit, a wedding morning — and score each for vividness, coherence and resale value. Recovered memories do not play like film. They re-render, slightly differently each time: faces almost stable, rooms breathing at the edges. At the Exchange that instability is a quality mark. It proves the lot is lived, not made.

The market has rules June can recite like scripture. A sale begins with extraction: the master memory is drawn into a sealed vial, and the seller's own recall fades to hearsay — you know it happened; you can no longer be there. Buyers receive a licensed playback keyed to the lot's provenance deed, the authenticated record of ownership, sale and consent. Copies made without the deed decay within a few plays, which is why the vault and registry — masters and deeds together — are the market: whoever controls authentication controls scarcity. Erasure, the shadow service, severs a playback from its deed and burns the buyer's retained impression; it cannot restore the original owner. Only recovering the master can. Adults may sell only what they can personally consent to. A minor's memories may be extracted only under a guardian's medical consent, certified as therapeutic — a narrow door, built for trauma care, wide enough to drive a childhood through.

June, 34, is the floor's most trusted cataloguer precisely because she never asks where the stock comes from. Her own childhood is the one ungraded lot in her life: an accident, a therapeutic wipe, nothing to recover. Her adult life is small and deliberately light: a tidy flat, one friendship kept at colleague distance, a supervisor who protects her because incuriosity like hers cannot be trained. Her brother Theo she barely sees; he remembers the childhood she does not, and their meetings have the temperature of a debt neither will name.

The inciting incident is a routine lot. Lot 1147: a seventh birthday, candles, a woman singing. June recognises the perspective with her body before her mind — she is looking down at a seven-year-old June Park through her mother's eyes. The provenance line is worse than the image. Seller: the guardian who raised her. Date: three months after the accident that supposedly wiped her in a day. Her childhood was not lost. It was extracted and sold — in what the registry, queried under a grading pretext, confirms were forty-one separate lots.

June does not report it. She buys it, at staff terms she cannot afford, and plays it alone after hours. The purchase puts her in debt to the house: an employee debtor's future wins are held as collateral against her balance. She has taken one step toward her past and become, in the Exchange's ledger, an asset recovering itself at her own expense.

Two facts plant themselves without emphasis. The Office of Memory Standards has announced an audit of the founding archive, and compliance staff are drilling burn protocols — the lawful destruction of unlawfully held masters ahead of seizure. And Odette Vesper, the founder, personally approves June's credit, asking only whether the lot was everything June had hoped. Odette believes, sincerely, that selling memory is mercy: the one asset the poor own that the rich cannot manufacture. She is not hiding June's truth from her. She is holding it, the way her house holds everything, until the claimant can pay what it costs.

To climb the buyer chain June hires Elias Vane, once the Exchange's best retrieval specialist — its most gifted thief of memories — now selling erasures to the desperate. He knows which rival house fences unlisted lots before she tells him they exist, and corrects her pronunciation of a buyer's name she has not yet spoken aloud. His knowledge keeps arriving one scene too early. He was present, in some capacity, the night her mother disappeared; he confirms this like a man confirming the weather, and declines to clear himself. June hires him anyway. He is the only map she has.

Act II — Escalation

The buyer chain is a heist conducted as a series of evictions. Each lot has been lived in by its buyer, sometimes for decades. The widower is the chain's conscience: he bought June's childhood summers to replace his own dead daughter's, and inside them he is not a thief but a father in warm light. Removing them would kill the part of his life that survived her. June wins them only by trading a recovered lot she had meant to keep.

A rival house fences three of June's lots off-book and prices her desperation like a currency trade. Elias's erasure clients begin recognising June in the street, and one asks her, with real tenderness, how her mother is. Each recovered lot contradicts the last: her mother is tender at the lake house, then desperate, then calm and methodical, packing like a woman executing a plan. The accident story dies early. What replaces it will not sit still.

Debt tightens the screw. Every win is collateral; the Exchange extends June's credit with a patience that begins to look like cultivation. It is. Odette is feeding the chain — releasing one lot June can reach, holding another back — because she has her own schedule for June, older than June's employment. When June names this, Odette does not deny it. A creditor, she says, wants the debt to mature.

Theo is Act II's locked door. June goes to her brother at last, not for a lot — his memories were never extracted — but for the family's past. He refuses her at any price, and the film explains the asymmetry the plot stands on. After their mother disappeared, the guardian certified June's extraction as therapy: a traumatised girl, compliant under sedation, session after session, until forty-one lots had been drawn out of her and sold over the following years to pay the household's debts. An extraction was attempted on Theo once. He fought the sedation, nearly died under it, and the clinic recorded him as unsafe to extract. The guardian never tried again — and told the boy, who remembered everything, that his sister had needed the wiping and he had failed to protect her. Theo's intact memory is not luck; it is the wound he was left holding. His refusal is not greed: he believes that if June recovers the past as property she will do what their mother did — edit it, price it, and leave him alone inside it again. His withholding is familial, not procedural.

June confronts the guardian himself, now old and living on the last of the proceeds. It is the film's coldest scene. He does not perform villainy. He itemises: the house would have been lost, the children taken into care, the clinic called it treatment — and besides, he says almost gently, their mother had already left them before he sold anything. She walked out. He merely monetised the wreckage. June leaves with no usable confession and one sentence she cannot unhear: perhaps her mother was not taken at all.

The midpoint reversal arrives inside the lot June has chased hardest: the last summer, the lake house, her mother's laughter on the porch. Playing it, she finds Elias in it — young, in Exchange uniform, carrying a sealed case out of the house while her mother watches and does not stop him. Her guide is inside the crime. Confronted, he asks one question: what does she think he was carrying? June has assumed the case held something taken from the house. Perhaps nothing was stolen that night. Perhaps something was delivered — and if Elias was a courier, the night she is hunting was not a theft scene but a transaction her mother arranged.

Act II ends in accelerating exposure. The audit is confirmed. The Exchange's board, not Odette alone, settles its defence: it will invite the raid on its own timetable, a controlled inspection during which the burn protocol will destroy the founding archive's masters — including records proving how many were extracted from minors under guardianship consent. The arson is institutional self-protection, prepared in board minutes June glimpses through Elias's old access. Odette signs it with a serenity June now understands is divided: the founder who built an empire on never destroying stock will burn her own archive to keep the empire. June, meanwhile, holds or has played forty of the forty-one lots. The forty-first has no buyer, no price and no listing. It never did. She goes to Odette, who has expected this appointment for twenty-six years.

Act III — Resolution

Odette reveals what she has held. The final lot was never sold, because it was never the Exchange's to sell. June's mother deposited it in a sealed trust under the founding charter: a master the house may hold but never trade, released only on the depositor's stated condition. The Exchange accepted those terms because a young house needed depositors to believe its vault was sacred — and because Odette, who had once sold a memory of her own grief and felt only relief, believed a depositor's condition was the one contract her philosophy could not break without becoming theft. She has honoured it for twenty-six years while building an empire on the opposite principle. The condition is precise: the night may be played only by June, and only when she has recovered enough of the rest to survive it. That is why Odette fed the chain. She was holding the truth for June, not hiding it.

The crisis assembles all three prices at once. The raid begins on the Exchange's arranged morning; the burn protocol engages in the founding vault, where thousands of strangers' masters count down toward lawful destruction. The trust lot sits in its own escrow cell, exempt from the burn but playable only here, only now: June can take her one night and walk out, or hold the vault for the strangers. Elias offers her both, at his price. He will use his old security authority to slow the protocol and open the escrow cell in the same window — if June lets his account go on the record: a sworn provenance deposition, admissible to the regulator, stating what he carried out of the lake house that night. What he carried was the deposit itself — the extracted night, its trust deed and his falsified log — for the mother who hired a frightened young security man, off the books, to carry it into a vault her children could one day claim. He has spent his adult life stealing memories, then erasing them, because he knew from that first job what the market did with what it was handed — and because the mother's condition bound him, as it bound Odette, not to tell the children before the claimant came. On the record, the deposition ends his deniability and livelihood. He pays it.

June chooses the archive. She and Elias hold the vault past the deadline — not by force but by provenance, flooding the registry with challenges that make every scheduled destruction legally contestable — long enough to copy the masters, with their deeds, into a mirrored registry no single house controls. She does not publish strangers' childhoods; doing to thousands of people what was done to her would be the guardian's logic with better branding. The copies go into escrow: masters sealed, deeds public, reclaimable only by the person the memory was taken from, or their heirs. What dies that night is not privacy. It is scarcity.

Only then does June play her final lot, last, in the emptied auction room, with Theo beside her — Theo, who came because she finally asked him to witness rather than to supply. The memory shows their mother leaving by choice: walking out into a new identity to escape the guardian, the man who controlled the household's money, its story and its future. She did not vanish and was never taken. She deposited the night as a timed message, proof for adulthood, because she believed a child's version of it would be taken from them too.

The film does not absolve her, and Theo is entitled to say so in the room. She escaped, and she left both children with the man she escaped from; the deposit was love and evidence, and it was not rescue. June does not argue. She lets the charge stand beside the proof — the first time either sibling has held both truths about their mother in the same hour. What their mother left them was not exoneration. It was the means to stop paying for her decision, and the guardian's, with each other.

The final scene is months later and deliberately unglamorous. June is establishing the reclaim registry: a grading floor, repurposed, where lots are checked for claimants instead of buyers. Odette, ruined, unrepentant and still serene, applies for a job there. She believes everything she always believed; she no longer owns the building in which it is true. June hires her — not as forgiveness or triumph, but as the film's last ethic: keeper and claimant will now do the holding differently, in the open, under rules neither controls alone. Some lots, June tells her, we grade together.

Main characters

June Park

Memory cataloguer, protagonist

A meticulous archivist who built her adult life on being uncurious. Recovering her childhood piece by piece forces her to become the kind of person who breaks into vaults.

Elias Vane

Retrieval specialist

Once the Exchange's best thief of memories, now selling erasures to its victims. He helps June for money, then for a reason he refuses to name: he was present the night her mother vanished.

Odette Vesper

Founder of the Vesper Exchange

A serene, terrifying businesswoman who believes selling memory is mercy. She has held June's final lot in a sealed trust for twenty-six years, deposited by June's mother, and has been waiting for June to come and claim it.

Theo Park

June's estranged brother

Theo remembers everything June lost and has built a bitter life around her forgetting. His refusal to sell a single memory makes him the Exchange's most wanted holdout.

Signature Scenes

The Grading of Lot 1147

A routine grading turns into the Exchange's most dangerous screening when June Park plays a seventh-birthday lot and finds herself inside it, watching her childhood self through her mother's eyes, with the seller's name on the provenance line.

The Widower's Summers

June must negotiate for her own childhood summers with a widower who bought them to survive his daughter's death, and learns that in this market even getting your past back is priced in someone else's grief.

The Lake-House Replay

Inside the lake-house summer she fought hardest to recover, June finds her own guide already there, years younger, carrying a sealed case out of her mother's house — and his refusal to explain it changes every question she has been asking.

The Emptied Auction Room

In the emptied auction room where her childhood was sold lot by lot, June finally plays the last memory with her brother beside her, and the two of them watch the night their mother disappeared together, as witnesses instead of buyers.
