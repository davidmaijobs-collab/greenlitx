One Hundred Winters

Some letters take a lifetime to arrive.

Separated by a border drawn through their valley in 1919, two lovers who can never meet again leave each other letters in the wall of a mountain lodge — a correspondence their grandchildren will finish a hundred years later.

Story Overview

Before the winter of 1919, the Brenner valley is one world. Its villages speak German and Italian across the same squares, its railway climbs to the pass, and at its heart stands a mountain lodge, built a generation earlier, where the whole valley mixes. Anna Wiesner teaches in the village school on the northern slope. Matteo Rinaldi, an engineer on the Brenner line, keeps its bridges and snow sheds standing. They meet at the lodge, over winters, the way valley people meet — gradually, then all at once. By the last winter of the old world, they are engaged.

Then the peace settlement after the Great War redraws the map of Europe, and the new Austria–Italy frontier is set along the Brenner watershed. The lodge stands south of it, in Italy. Anna's village stands north of it, in Austria. Matteo is ordered south with the railway; Anna's family, her school and her whole spoken world are on the other side. Papers, languages and loyalties harden around a valley that had never needed them. On the last night before winter closes the high road, Matteo shows Anna a stone in the hearth that has always been loose, and leaves the first letter inside it. Not a farewell — a schedule: once a year, when the road opens, a letter will be waiting.

Within two years each is married to someone else — Anna to a kind schoolmaster in her own village, Matteo into a railway family of the new Italy — and both marriages are honoured, fully, without secret meetings or secret plans. What survives is the schedule. A letter is written across each year like a diary, and carried to the lodge when the year's journey can be made. Matteo arranges postings and inspections along the Brenner line so that once a year, professionally and innocently, his work passes the lodge. Anna journeys from the north. They are never in the room together: each leaves a letter, and takes away the one that has waited.

The letters become the valley's secret history of the century. Regimes rename the frontier's stations and police its languages. War turns the Brenner road military and closes it to people like them for years; the letters for those years are written anyway, kept, and delivered late, in bundles. Children are born, and grow up on a version of their parents' story with the centre removed. Spouses age, and die. Through all of it the lodge stands, redressed by every decade — new owners, new paint, new purposes — while its hearth, and the loose stone in it, remains where two young people left it in 1919.

In 1985 the lodge is being restored, and the architect in charge of the work is Clara Wiesner, Anna's granddaughter. Clara's trade is buildings that outlast the people who made them, and she took this commission with her own family's recent losses still raw. The restoration plans require the old hearth to be dismantled and rebuilt, and Clara's own survey — her hands on the stones, checking what can be saved — finds the loose stone, and behind it, in the dark of the wall, more than sixty years of letters, in two hands and two languages, laid down one year at a time.

The letters answer each other across the wall, but half of the conversation belongs to a family across a border that no longer frightens anyone. In the railway archive at Bolzano works Davide Rinaldi, Matteo's grandson, keeper of his grandfather's papers — a quiet man carrying a family obligation he has never explained to anyone. The letters keep asking Clara a harder question: whether peace built on an omission is peace at all, and what the living owe a love that asked for nothing but one letter a year. An epic romance told at the speed of seasons, in snow, firelight and handwriting.

The story

In the winter of 1919, a new border is drawn through the Brenner valley, and through the engagement of Anna Wiesner, an Austrian schoolteacher, and Matteo Rinaldi, an Italian railway engineer. The mountain lodge where they met is suddenly in another country. Each will marry someone else. Neither will stop writing. Once a year, for sixty-one years, a letter waits in a loose stone of the lodge's hearth — and in 1985 Anna's granddaughter, restoring that lodge, finds them all.

One Hundred Winters

Development treatment — not a shooting script.

Act I — Setup (1918–1921)

The lodge comes first, because the lodge outlasts everyone. It was built in the summer of 1886, an inn below the Brenner road, and the film will end in its hundredth winter. Its hearth is valley stone, and one stone in it, at shoulder height beside the fire, has been loose since before anyone in the film was born. Anna Wiesner, born in 1889, teaches in the village school on the northern slope. At twenty-nine she is a schoolteacher with her own salary and classroom, and a dry, exact way of speaking that her pupils imitate and her mother deplores. Matteo Rinaldi, born in 1887, is an engineer on the Brenner railway, a builder of bridges and snow sheds across the very mountains that are about to divide them. His courtship of Anna is conducted in his native language, which is work: he mends the lodge's guttering, rehangs its door, and, one evening, shows her the hearth stone that rocks under his hand — a stone that comes out, leaving a dry, dark pocket in the wall.

They become engaged at Christmas 1918, in a valley that believes the worst is finished. It is not. In 1919 the post-war settlement assigns the land south of the Brenner watershed to Italy, and the published line runs through the valley: the lodge stands in Italy, Anna's village in Austria. Matteo is ordered south with the railway's Italian administration. Anna's family closes around her like weather — an Italian fiancé is now a foreign official, her school post depends on respectability, and the valley, overnight, has a side, and she is on the other one. Neither family is cruel; that is the wound. Everyone is reasonable, and reasonableness is enough.

On the last night before winter closes the high road, Matteo performs the film's founding act: he takes the loose stone out of the hearth, leaves the first letter in the pocket behind it, and replaces the stone. It is not a farewell, and he forbids her to treat it as one. It is a schedule. Once a year, when the road opens, a letter will be waiting. Anna reads it by firelight as his train crosses into another country. Within two years each is married to someone else: Anna, in 1920, to Josef, a kind schoolmaster in her own village who suspects nothing and deserves better; Matteo, in 1921, to Lucia, of a railway family building the new Italy. The marriages are real. The schedule is also real. The film's whole life is the narrow ground between those two sentences.

Act II — Escalation (1921–1980, braided with 1985)

The braid is the structure. The correspondence runs forward through the century while, in 1985, Clara reads it backward into order, rebuilding the room that held it; every decade it uncovers is one the restoration strand must handle and lay out on the lodge floor.

The ritual has rules, and the rules are the romance. A letter is written across the whole year and carried to the lodge each spring when the road to it opens. The writer leaves the new letter behind the stone and takes away the one that has waited. They are never in the room together; the room is the meeting. Matteo arranges his entire career around the Brenner line — postings, inspections, bridge works — so that once a year, professionally and innocently, he can pass the lodge. His letters are written the way he builds: plain, exact, load-bearing. Weather on the line. What the winter took. What he mended. Affection arrives as measurement and fact, all the more devastating for never naming itself. Anna's letters are the film's conscience: dry, funny, unsentimental, devastating in what they refuse to say. She writes about the schoolroom, the village, the marriage she honours — and the honouring is not a pose: she will not make Josef a fool in prose any more than in life.

The century tests the schedule, and the schedule bends without breaking. In the 1920s the border hardens into documents, languages and suspicions. Under fascism the Brenner becomes a showcase frontier and the Italian side polices the valley's German names and tongues; Matteo, building for the Italian railway, builds what the regime's railway needs. In the 1930s a letter is opened and read by other eyes before it reaches the wall, and its most dangerous sentence is a date — the innocent date of a school fair, read by a censor as a rendezvous — and for one spring the correspondence comes within a single official question of ending everything. They survive it by becoming, on the surface, even plainer — two old acquaintances exchanging valley news — and the love moves entirely into what the letters refuse to say.

War closes the road to people like them for four consecutive years. Those years' letters are written anyway, kept in drawers on both sides of a military frontier, and delivered late, in a bundle, at the first opening: a letter for every year, though not every letter arrived in its year. The first post-war exchange contains the correspondence's only quarrel. Anna's 1946 letter is short and cold. You built for them, she writes. Tell me what you built. Matteo's reply takes most of a year and does not exonerate him: he lists the works, what the line carried, what he told himself a railwayman owes the trains, and where that reasoning now shames him. She does not offer absolution. What she offers, the following spring, is the next letter — ordinary, wry, about a roof and a christening — and in their grammar that is the forgiveness: continuation. Against that quarrel the film sets an ordinary year, 1957, in which nothing happens at all: a roof mended, a grandchild expected, soup, weather. Anna writes four pages about it, because devotion that survives an ordinary year can survive anything except time.

Spouses die — Josef in 1963, Lucia in 1968 — and the widowed correspondents, in their seventies, do not turn the letters into the courtship they renounced at thirty. The discipline holds. Then age does what armies could not. Around 1970 Matteo's hands begin to fail him; the engineer's fluent, ruled handwriting stumbles. He does the most Matteo thing available: he finds an instrument. His grandson Davide, born in 1950, is twenty, training in the railway's records service at Bolzano, and devoted to his grandfather. Matteo asks him to write to dictation, and tells him only that the letters are a debt of honour owed to an old friend across the border. He does not tell him the whole truth — it is not his alone to give away.

Davide learns it anyway: an amanuensis reads both sides of a correspondence. Through the 1970s he takes down his grandfather's dictation, carries the letters north on the annual journey once Matteo can no longer climb, and collects Anna's replies — and reads sixty years of a woman writing. The dictation becomes composition by degrees neither man announces: a sentence finished, then a paragraph drafted for failing eyes to approve, then whole letters composed in a studied voice. Matteo dies in the winter of 1976, aged eighty-nine. The correspondence should end there. Davide, twenty-six, ashamed even as he does it, writes one more year. Then one more. He tells himself each spring that this is the last, and each spring he cannot let the letter waiting in the wall go unanswered into silence.

Anna's replies of those years show no surprise. In 1973 she writes a single sentence — Give my regards to the hand that helps you — and never mentions it again. She knows the voice has changed; the sentences have shortened, the jokes have become someone else's, kindly worn like a borrowed coat. The question stops being did they stay faithful, and becomes to what — a person, or the writing itself?

In the winter of 1980, Anna, ninety-one, makes one last journey outside the rhythm: a second letter in a single year, left behind the stone in December, after the season's exchange is complete. It is a bequest, not an instalment. She dies in February 1981. Davide, preparing that spring's reply, hears of her death — and believes the wall is empty, the last exchange already made. He never climbs to the lodge again. The final letter waits, uncollected, through four more winters.

The 1985 strand gathers all of it. Clara Wiesner, born in 1951, is Anna's granddaughter — daughter of Anna's daughter Maria, who grew up, married and died in 1983 on a version of her mother's story with the centre removed. Clara is a restorations architect, thirty-four, precise and protective, and she took the lodge commission partly as a return: her mother's valley, her grandmother's building, grief she can work on at one remove. Her survey of the hearth — which the owners intend to dismantle for a marketable redesign — finds the loose stone. The owners have already made that intention an argument in person, at Clara's first inspection of the lodge, standing with her in the cold hearth room before the work began: the hearth as it stands, they told her, is the one thing in the building that cannot be sold, and it will have to come down and be rebuilt prettier; Clara argued then only that the stonework be recorded before it was touched, and she has surveyed it ever since against that priced position — a hearth worth more rebuilt than true. Laying sixty years of letters out in order across the restoration floor, she sees what single letters conceal and a row reveals: in 1970 the voice changes. Clara's family wants the letters sealed unread. The owners want a marketable history. And there is, her survey tells her, a weight and a fold in the wall that the opened cache does not account for — one more letter, still behind the stone.

Act III — Resolution (1985)

Clara crosses to the Italian side — an ordinary journey now, passports and a train, which is its own quiet indictment of 1919 — and finds Davide in the railway archive at Bolzano. The confrontation is not a swoon but an audit. She lays the evidence out in order, as he once laid out dictation: the shortened sentences, the borrowed jokes, the decade of answers written in a dead man's name to a woman who deserved the truth. Davide admits everything: the dictation that became composition, the years of telling himself one more spring, and the thing he is most ashamed of — that somewhere in the decade he stopped writing only for Matteo. He has fallen, impossibly and entirely, for Anna Wiesner, through sixty years of her letters, a woman he never met, who died when he was thirty. Clara's judgment is exact and not kind: You did not keep a correspondence alive, she tells him. You kept a woman writing to a ghost, because the ghost's mail was the only place you were loved in a voice you trusted. It does not end in forgiveness. It ends with Clara telling him what is still in the wall, and what happens to the wall in the morning.

For the owners have moved the hearth's dismantling forward. The stone comes down at first light, by contractors, as rubble — and Anna's final letter, the December bequest no one collected, will go into a skip unread unless it is recovered tonight, in winter, up a mountain road that is closing. Recovering it means defying the owners, her family's wish for silence, and the terms of Clara's own commission. Clara chooses the letter. Davide chooses to come with her: the first honest journey either family has made to that lodge, and the first time in sixty-six years anyone from the two families has climbed to it side by side.

They work the loose stone free together, in the firelit room where the schedule began, and Clara reads Anna's final letter aloud. It is clear-eyed and unsentimental, aware that the answering hand had changed, and grateful anyway. I knew whose hand it was, she writes, in substance. It was a kind hand, and it kept its word. We were faithful to two lives and one letter a year. Do not repeat us. Meet. The letter does not forgive Davide from beyond the grave; it refuses to let forgiveness be the point. Anna, who never wasted a sentence, spends her last one on an instruction to the living. Clara reads it twice; the second time, she reads it as addressed to her.

What follows is done in the open, which is how the film knows it is an ending and not another secret. Davide writes the reply that was never written — the answer to Anna's final letter — and for the first time in fifteen years of writing he writes as himself, signing his own name, and leaves it behind the stone beside hers. Shown two pages — the first letter of 1919 and the last of 1980 — the owners understand what they actually own: not a picturesque hearth to be modernised, but the one marketable, unrepeatable thing in the valley, a true wall. The hearth is saved, preserved as it stands, stone loose and all, and the letters pass into the keeping of both families jointly. Clara's commission ends in a building kept honest rather than made pretty, and her family ends the film in possession of its own centre.

Final image, the lodge's hundredth winter: snow on the pass, the lodge lit against it, and two sets of footprints crossing the yard together, side by side, where a border used to be — which is to say, nowhere visible at all. Clara and Davide part in daylight, in their own names, having met. What they will be to each other is the film's last, deliberate blank page — Anna's instruction obeyed, not her story repeated.

Main characters

Anna Wiesner

Schoolteacher, co-protagonist

Pragmatic, unsentimental, and faithful to two lives at once: the marriage she honors and the correspondence she never misses. Her letters are the film's conscience.

Matteo Rinaldi

Railway engineer, co-protagonist

A builder of bridges across the very mountains that divide them. He engineers his whole career around the Brenner line so that once a year, professionally, innocently, he can pass the lodge.

Clara Wiesner

Granddaughter, restorations architect

Restoring the lodge in 1985, she finds the hearth letters and must decide whether a family's peace is worth more than its truth.

Davide Rinaldi

Grandson

A quiet archivist in the Bolzano railway archive, keeper of his grandfather's papers and of a family promise he has never been able to explain to anyone.

Signature Scenes

The First Letter

On the last night before winter closes the high road in 1919, Matteo Rinaldi works a loose stone free of the lodge hearth, leaves the first letter in the dark behind it, and replaces the stone — turning a farewell into an appointment neither of them will ever break.

The Bundle of the War Years

For four war years the Brenner road is closed to people like them, so the letters are written anyway and kept in drawers on both sides of the frontier — and at the first opening, four years of devotion change hands through the hearth wall in a single bundle.

Sixty Years Laid Out in Order

In the stripped, scaffolded lodge of 1985, Clara Wiesner lays more than sixty years of her grandmother's letters out in order across the restoration floor and reads the century in sequence, in the very room whose hearth kept it, while the building waits to be changed around her.

The Night Climb and the Loose Stone

On the last night before contractors take the hearth down, Clara Wiesner and Davide Rinaldi climb through closing snow to the dark lodge together — the first time anyone from either family has made that journey side by side — and work the loose stone free to recover what still waits in the wall.
